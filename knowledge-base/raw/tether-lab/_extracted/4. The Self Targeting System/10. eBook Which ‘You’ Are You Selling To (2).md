## Page 3

Actual Self
Who I am right now.
Seeks validation, consistency, and identity alignment. Ads that reinforce
this self feel comforting and familiar.
Example: “Finally, cookware that gets you.”
Ideal Self
Who I want to be.
Chases growth, pride, and emotional reward. Ads here feel aspirational
and exciting.
Example: “Cook like the version of you who doesn’t cut corners.”
Self-Discrepancy Theory, introduced by psychologist E. Tory Higgins,
reveals that our emotions and behaviors are deeply influenced by the gap
between different versions of ourselves.

## Page 4

Ought Self
Who I think I should be.
Driven by duty, pressure, or social expectation. These ads act like
responsible reminders.
Example: “You know you should be taking better care of yourself.”

Each self creates a different kind of emotional pull. When your ad doesn’t
match the customer’s dominant self, it falls flat. When it does? It converts.

## Page 9

Here’s a quick guide to matching your message to your customer’s
mindset:
Self-to-Funnel Match Guide
Awareness: Use Ideal or Ought. Tap into emotion or urgency.
Consideration: Use Actual or Ideal. Build trust or show
transformation.
Conversion: Use Actual or Ought. Anchor in comfort or push with
guilt.
Think of it like this:
Ideal: Pull them in with possibility.
Actual: Calm their nerves with familiarity.
Ought: Nudge them off the fence with pressure or logic.

Every brand leans into one self-type more than others. It happens
subconsciously. But when you spot it, you can balance it.
Use the Ad Self-Score Worksheet (Chapter 3) across 10–20 recent ads.
Look for your highest average score.
Then ask:
Are we defaulting to Ideal and ignoring Actual?
Are we overusing guilt or pressure?
Do our top performers align with our target self-type?
