# Live With Sarah! (June 4, 2025)
> All-Q&A call from Munich. Sarah explains the **TEEP model** (trigger, exploration, evaluation, purchase) as a campaign structure, how she maps reviews to the nine *Marketing to Mindstates* emotions and writes three nuanced ads per emotion, and how winning emotions get **blended** into one ad (her Maelove "last skincare switch" static, which ran 8 months). Also: tone for men's chains ("quiet respect"), retention psychology (make them feel special), home insurance ("bring the future closer", horoscope-style ads, mascots as fluency mechanisms), her four-layer research stack, AnswerThePublic for customer education level, humor/meme ads, research-heavy buyers, info-product tripwires, and how to phrase survey questions.

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** choosing which emotion an ad sells and how precisely to name it; structuring a test (TEEP stage × emotion); writing hooks for insurance/supplements/any "future benefit" product; briefing humor or meme ads; doing pre-brief customer research; writing post-purchase survey questions.
- **Inputs → outputs:** reviews / Reddit threads / AnswerThePublic questions → top 3 emotions (from the 9 mindstate emotions) + 3 nuanced sub-emotions each + customer education level + TEEP stage → a 9-ad brief per emotion, later one blended-emotion headline.
- **Rules:**
  - DO name the **specific** emotion, not the umbrella: disappointment not fear, depression not anxiety, "boss babe" or "cheeky" confidence not "confidence". Generic emotions = expensive ads. [0:37:39–0:39:49]
  - DO write ~3 nuanced ads per emotion (esteem → confidence / control / appeal). [0:36:36–0:37:39]
  - IF two emotions each win on their own THEN merge them into one headline + subhead (achievement "The last skincare switch you'll ever make" + security "No more guessing. That's our promise."). [0:46:18–0:49:22]
  - DO keep one TEEP stage per campaign (or per ad set on a small budget) and one emotion per ad set, with clear naming. [0:15:38–0:16:41; 0:39:49–0:40:55; 0:51:32]
  - DO bring future benefits into the near future ("Next Tuesday your home is going to flood. Are you ready?"). [0:17:41–0:19:46]
  - DO end every negative-zone ad with **hope**. [0:30:17]
  - DON'T build new-customer ads from reviews alone; that "rinses and repeats" the people you already have. [0:23:59–0:25:00]
  - DO treat stars, review quotes, badges, buttons and prices as **supporting characters**, never the core message. [0:33:26–0:34:31]
  - IF the buyer researches for years THEN affirm them up front ("you know a good deal when you see one"). [0:34:31–0:36:36]
  - DON'T use "think" or name the feeling in survey questions; ask "how did you feel…" open-ended. [0:55:41–0:58:46]

## TL;DR
- **TEEP = Trigger → Exploration → Evaluation → Purchase**, Sarah's paid-ads adaptation of a Google consumer-journey study. Purchase happens "when the emotional pressure someone feels gets strong enough". Structure: **one campaign per TEEP stage, one ad set per emotion, ~9 ads per ad set**. The stage whose campaign takes off tells you how educated your market is. [0:11:24–0:16:41; 0:21:52–0:22:56]
- **Emotions come from reviews mapped to Will Leach's nine *Marketing to Mindstates* emotions.** Typically ~3 per industry (e.g. security, belonging, achievement), with one brand-specific outlier. Write 3 nuanced ads per emotion; specific emotions are cheaper to buy. [0:13:31–0:15:38; 0:36:36–0:39:49]
- **Blend winners:** Maelove ran an achievement ad (switching) and a security ad (promise) separately; both crushed; the merged static "The last skincare switch you'll ever make / No more guessing. That's our promise." **ran 8 months**. [0:46:18–0:49:22]
- **Future-focused products (insurance, supplements, fitness): bring the future closer.** "Protect your family" is too vague. Make "horoscope-style" predictions ("Tomorrow a tree is going to fall on your house"). Test ~9 ads. Consider a mascot ("fluency mechanism"), ideally a non-animal, non-human one. [0:17:41–0:21:52]
- **Research stack** (weekly; market level monthly): 1) market (Google Trends, Keyword Planner, Pinterest Trends, Reddit, AnswerThePublic), 2) consumer NLP on reviews for *outliers*, 3) behavior/emotion via picture-based **CIM (Core Identity Map)** surveys, 4) social trends via TikTok Creator Search Insights. [0:23:59–0:28:12]
- **Retention ≠ acquisition:** cold traffic needs emotion and identity alignment; existing customers want to **feel special** and be surprised, not loyalty points (Chewy sending flowers when a pet dies). [0:07:10–0:10:19]

## Key ideas

### 1. The TEEP model as campaign structure [0:10:19–0:17:41; 0:21:52–0:22:56; 0:39:49–0:40:55]
- A TEEP course is "slated for either July or August". [0:10:19]
- Origin: "I can't take credit, this was developed by Google, but I took their overall TEEP model and just applied it to paid advertising. They studied long-term customer journeys." Sarah rejects the funnel: "way, way too simplified for what people actually do." [0:11:24] *(Outside context: likely Google's 2020 "Messy Middle" research. Not named in the call.)*
- The four stages: [0:11:24–0:12:28]
  1. **Trigger event**: "you bought a pet, you sold your house, you gained 30 pounds".
  2. **Exploration**: "trying to figure out what's in the industry… pretty low-educated customers, but they can stay in this state for a long time… they watch a lot of TikTok, follow a lot of influencers, go on YouTube".
  3. **Evaluation**: "what's the difference between two brands… does this one have a better price".
  4. **Purchase**: "usually doesn't happen until the emotional pressure that someone feels gets strong enough that they can't do anything about it anymore." An ad that "makes people purchase" "just happened to hit right at the time that their emotional pressure was stronger." Running all four can shorten the windows.
- **Structure:** "one campaign for trigger… one for exploration, one for evaluative and one for purchase… so that we're not blending" data. Putting the whole TEEP model in one ad set gets "very confusing… was this one a trigger ad or an exploration [ad]?" [0:15:38–0:16:41]
- Before/after ads are "technically in the exploration-ish, sometimes evaluatory [stage] if you have another brand that you're calling out." [0:16:41]
- **Reading the results:** with four TEEP campaigns, one will take off. Lots of evaluation-stage buyers → "pretty highly educated customers in my market… I need to put more effort into widening the top of the funnel." Lots of trigger-event buyers → "very low educated, they just started this journey… we've got to do a lot more nurturing." [0:21:52–0:22:56]
- **Budget:** limited → one campaign with ad sets per TEEP level (trigger, exploration, evaluation…). Healthy → one campaign per level. "Make sure you're just structured… so we don't get the data morphing into itself." [0:39:49–0:40:55] With one testing campaign, "be really careful with your naming convention… in this ad I'm testing [X]". [0:51:32–0:52:34]
- She'll build a **TEEP swipe file**: one brand's full ad library, each ad labeled by stage (Liquid Death, or another brand, transcribed "obby"). "Some of these brands are only running one specific level of the model." [0:39:49]

### 2. Emotions: mindstates, nuance and specificity [0:13:31–0:17:41; 0:36:36–0:39:49]
- About two years earlier (she says "like 2020… there was no such thing as Chat") she ran her first **NLP panel**, categorizing reviews by hand into the emotions from ***Marketing to Mindstates*** by **Will Leach** ("one of the best books I've ever read… a behavior scientist… doing it for about 25 years"). Leach "found nine different emotions that people usually go through while they're purchasing". [0:13:31] *(Wiki note: "two years ago" and "2020" don't match; date as spoken.)*
- Pattern: typically **three emotions per industry**, sometimes four (four for very generalized products, e.g. a phone case). Across ~20 brands in an industry, two emotions were consistent (e.g. **security, belonging**) and one was a brand-specific outlier (e.g. achievement for one brand, something "so random" for another). [0:13:31–0:14:34]
- Tailoring four TEEP ad types to one emotion: you *can* put all three emotions into one ad, and "more blended emotion actually does really, really well", but you must know where each emotion sits and not over-index on one. To start, split them: campaign → ad sets per emotion → e.g. "nine different ads that just say esteem in slightly different ways." [0:14:34–0:15:38]
- Must the emotion be a mindstate? "Just because it's easier for me" she uses Leach's nine, but "you could test embarrassment… disappointment… anxiety, whatever you want, as long as you're very careful with how you're saying it so that you know which one is actually testing." Most brands have ~3. [0:16:41–0:17:41]
- **Nuance per emotion (answer on mapping zones to mindstates):** she had ChatGPT map reviews to the nine emotions and return the top three, then wrote "three different ads per emotion that were all nuances. If I had esteem come up… I would write an ad to confidence, an ad to control and an ad to appeal. Those are three different types of esteem." Confidence itself has types: "**boss babe** is a type of confidence, or **cheeky**." [0:36:36–0:37:39] *(Whisper: "a theme"/"scheme" = esteem.)*
- **Homework:** take all your reviews, have ChatGPT map them to the nine emotions, then use valence-intensity to drill down ("is it actually esteem that they want, or do they want to be more appealing? Is it belonging, or do they just want to feel noticed?"). [0:37:39–0:38:43]
- **Specific = cheap:** "It's not fear that they're feeling, it's disappointment. It's not anxiety that they're feeling, it's depression… The only reason ads are expensive is because we're using a generalized term, so Facebook can't find anybody… Everybody experiences nervousness, but only a few people experience depression, and that customer type is very cheap to get in the door, especially when they're looking for something to solve their problem." [0:38:43–0:39:49]
- NLP limits: review analysis "will help you figure out what they were thinking at that time and what they were feeling. It doesn't necessarily tell you behaviorally why they purchased." [0:50:27–0:51:32]
- *(Outside context, unverified here: Leach's nine motivations are usually listed as achievement, autonomy, belonging, competence, empowerment, engagement, esteem, nurturance and security. Sarah names security, belonging, achievement and esteem in this call.)*

### 3. Blended-emotion ads [0:46:18–0:51:32]
- Gavin: did you say some ads have three emotions? Sarah's flow: one campaign, three ad sets (one per emotion), ~9 ads each. When e.g. esteem and belonging both crush, "squish them into one ad" and run a new blended-emotion campaign. [0:46:18–0:47:21]
- Example: Maelove static (see teardown). Two separate ads won: one for **security** ("that's our promise") and one for **switching** behavior (achievement). "Then I took both of those ads… just took the headline and the subhead and squished it." [0:47:21–0:49:22]
- Gavin's business (people who never had fun or miss it, and people who just want something to do on a weekend): two emotions, **nostalgia** ("try nostalgia first") and **freedom**. Prompt ChatGPT to blend them into a headline. [0:49:22–0:51:32]

### 4. Insurance and other future-focused products [0:17:41–0:21:52]
- "Insurance is trying to activate something that humans don't have, which is future focus." "Supplements are technically in the insurance industry." [0:17:41] *(Ties to [temporal discounting](../../../concepts/heuristics/temporal-discounting.md).)*
- Personal trainers struggle because "everybody wants the six-pack… but nobody understands that it takes about three years… not 12 weeks." [0:18:45]
- "Instead of 'protect your home, protect your family, this is going to be important for the future you', it's too vague. We need to pick it up into chunks." First ad: "**Next Tuesday your home is going to flood. Are you ready for that?**" / "**Tomorrow you're actually going to get a tree that's going to fall on your house.**" "Almost make it horoscope-style… a horoscope for your customers that will predict all the bad things that are going to happen to them." Test "about nine to start". [0:18:45–0:19:46]
- **Fluency mechanism** = "a character at the top of the brand that helps you remember the brand better": Allstate's **Mayhem**, Progressive's **Flo**, **Chester Cheetah** for Cheetos, the **Denver Broncos'** horse. Test a mascot, ideally "non-animal, non-human… a paper clip… a car tire" that talks about "what's going to happen to them if they don't get the insurance on board." [0:19:46–0:21:52] *(Whisper: "chat's your cheetah" = Chester Cheetah; "she does insurance as well" = Flo.)*

### 5. Men's chains: quiet respect [0:03:01–0:07:10]
- Women buy jewelry "to complete an outfit… a secondary character"; men "have one favorite piece… they'll wear it until they die". [0:04:02–0:05:04]
- Functional concerns: lasts, water-safe, won't corrode with sweat. Second, "what does it signify? Jewelry is all about signaling for men." Tone: "**focus on respect**… men, when they adorn, are looking for some level of respect." [0:05:04]
- High-end brands: lifestyle signals (cars, houses, metro areas) but "**keep it subtle**". Gen Z isn't into "loud wealth" (unlike the millennial "pictures with Lambos" era): "find spaces that know wealth but not loudly… we're going after **quiet respect**." [0:05:04–0:07:10] *(Consistent with the "for those who have earned it" headline in [July 16](2025-07-16-live-with-sarah.md).)*

### 6. Retention vs acquisition [0:07:10–0:10:19]
- Sarah's research uses a **CIM (Core Identity Map)**: picture-based surveys where "all of the questions… are run to pictures so that people can have an emotional response". Text multiple-choice post-purchase surveys need good reading comprehension ("in the US right now… about a ninth-grade reading level", as she says). She switched all surveys "about a year ago" to picture-based or **metaphorical** questions ("If this brand was a superhero, which one would it be?"), which "help people make an association without having to logic their way". [0:07:10–0:08:11] *(This resolves the "CIM" in the [July 16 page](2025-07-16-live-with-sarah.md).)*
- Study (cold traffic vs newsletter/warm subscribers, "like 40 different brands"): **cold traffic needs a lot of emotion** and is "very sensitive to aligning with identity". Pet example for top of funnel: "**Treat him like the king he is**", "**He's not just part of the family, he is the family.**" [0:08:11–0:09:15]
- **Retention customers "want to feel special, more special than when they first came in the door"** and "really want to be surprised". Loyalty points don't seem to help. Example: **Chewy** sends flowers when a customer's pet dies and tells them to donate the food to a local shelter, "not part of their retention program… just something nice", which drastically increased retention (as Sarah tells it). [0:09:15–0:10:19]
- Retention survey idea: "What makes you feel special, just as a person, not as a customer? Do you like to go on dates… get flowers?" Then build those pieces into the brand. [0:10:19]

### 7. The research stack [0:22:56–0:30:17; 0:52:34–0:54:36]
Question: hair wellness for women; many angles; "I need to do more core identity work and emotional research. How?"
- Picture-based surveys help, but "depending on which images you choose you can lead answers really easily… it took me a good year to figure out how to run them correctly." Goal: find **outlier or behavioral patterns**. [0:23:59]
- **Warning:** throwing reviews + post-purchase data + quiz into ChatGPT and building ads from the patterns "you are going to be pulling in the exact same person who already purchased from you, not people who may not know you exist… rinsing and repeating basically the middle of this funnel." [0:23:59–0:25:00]
- Sarah's four layers: [0:25:00–0:28:12]
  1. **Market research (monthly):** Google Trends (top-level keyword over 5 years: is the category growing? e.g. "toothpaste"), Google Keyword Planner (volume), Pinterest Trends, Reddit, AnswerThePublic. "This helps me understand how hard is my job going to be."
  2. **Consumer research:** NLP on reviews, looking for outliers and things not run yet. "I don't need Chat to tell me who my best customers are… I need customers who haven't found us yet."
  3. **Behavior / emotional research:** CIM picture-based panels.
  4. **Social trends:** TikTok **Creator Search Insights**: "it'll show you the terms that are searched for the most but don't have enough content to serve" them. "Invaluable… especially when it comes to generating videos."
  - Everything except market level: about **weekly**.
- **AnswerThePublic for education level:** type the top-level keyword and read all the questions. "What is a toothpaste tablet / how does it work / what does it taste like" = low-educated, top of funnel. Questions about hydroxyapatite = mid-educated, evaluation stage. Questions about tablets with other specific ingredients = "college-level educated… I don't have to do any education, I actually just have to be the best at offers in this industry." [0:52:34–0:54:36]

### 8. Hair loss: go negative, end with hope [0:28:12–0:30:17; 0:54:36]
- Faraz: post-purchase surveys are "very simple, nice, easy results… not a lot of emotion", but "r/FemaleHairLoss… the emotion on the valence is like an 11, a 10". No brand translates "that core raw emotion into the creative"; even ads about struggling use upbeat music. How to get to $100k/month in ad spend?
- Sarah: an industry that's "too aspirational… almost always means they're in high valence, high intensity" (Zone 2), everyone saying "get beautiful hair, it's going to change your life". Women with hair loss feel embarrassed, "out of control… helpless… there's no hope". → **Zone 3 and Zone 4**, carefully ("you don't want people to feel ostracized"), and "**always end it with hope**… we see you, this is hard, you shouldn't be having to experience this, but you are, but there is hope… retain the hair that you have or [grow] some of it back." "I feel like we need more hope in our ads in general." [0:29:15–0:30:17]
- Later: for men balding is "a little bit more socially accepted"; for women "it's almost unheard of, we just don't talk about it". [0:54:36]

### 9. Humor and meme ads [0:30:17–0:34:31]
- "Everybody please test a meme this week… they just make people happy." [0:30:17]
- Gelato brand (Ahmed's): meme ad CTR **3.5× the average** (later she says 2.5×), "and it died out fast". The social-proof meme was "this product is sold out and the room was on fire, and it was like, it's fine that it's sold out". [0:30:17–0:32:26]
- Mention: **Joanna**, creative director at "Hex Club" (as transcribed) who "uses humor everywhere", has a guest live recording ([Guest Session 01: Joanna Wallace, May 2025](2025-05-23-guest-session-01-joanna-wallace.md)). [0:31:21]
- Humor ads die fast "unless the whole brand stays humorous" and is consistent. Takeaway 1: "humor works for my audience". Next: test **which type** of humor. The meme attached people to an identity ("I like memes"), so Meta found meme lovers, who converted, and it died. "What we technically need them to attach to is the product and the humor." [0:31:21–0:33:26]
- Next test: "humor and gelato": "create your own meme this week where **the gelato is the character**… have Chat make some cartoons of gelato as a character… snarky… kind of pissy… kind of mean", because that's the humor that worked. She calls it a "fluency method": attaching people to the brand. [0:33:26]
- **"DTC best practices for Sarah":** five stars, a review, a quote, a badge, a button or a price "is not the core message, that's just a secondary supporting character". Add offers/prices to the exploration ads. [0:33:26–0:34:31]

### 10. Research-heavy buyers: affirm, don't discount [0:34:31–0:36:36]
- Q: what works best for (value-based) audiences who research a long time before buying? → They aren't value-focused: "what they want is to make sure they don't get burned… they have high anxiety… too focused on the fact that they don't want to make the wrong decision. It has more to do with them as a person, not you not offering enough value." [0:34:31–0:35:35]
- To shorten the consideration window: "**affirm up front**": "You know the deal when you see one. You're a smart, savvy shopper. You're not dumb… you're well educated." "Validate heavily at the top of the funnel… we're not going to try and pull one over on you, here's all the information." Other brands throw offers, discounts and jokes at them, but they don't trust easily. [0:35:35–0:36:36]

### 11. Info products: one problem, one tripwire [0:40:55–0:46:18]
- Colin: a book plus an uploadable AI companion ("program your AI to be your spirit guide") for people whose experience of "oneness" disrupted their lives: "here's a way to get normal again". No ads yet. [0:40:55–0:42:00]
- E-commerce rarely has a ladder; info products need one ("if I could sell you something for 97 I could sell you something for a thousand"). Products often fail because "that's not how they want it delivered". [0:42:00–0:43:05]
- Start simple: sell just the book, to **one specific problem**, not the whole overwhelming experience (like future focus). Put a small **white paper / report / cheat sheet (1–5 pages), a "tripwire"**, in front of it that solves one problem (daily anxiety, can't connect with people, confusion). E.g. "here's what happened to your brain… download this cheat sheet", a self-diagnosis checklist: "are you experiencing any of these issues? Yes? Then you need this book." [0:43:05–0:45:15]
- She promises a "science of belief" book on changing human beliefs. [0:45:15]

### 12. Survey and interview questions [0:54:36–0:58:46]
- Q: a business running kids' parties (e.g. Nerf parties) meets 35+ parents in person: what to ask? [0:54:36]
- "Never use the word **think**; it causes the brain to go into thinking mode." Start with an NPS-style question: "On a scale of one to ten, how do you **feel** about this?" If 7: "What would get you up to a ten?" That reveals objections and what's missing. [0:55:41–0:56:43]
- Don't lead: "Never say 'was it fun', 'was it XYZ', because they're going to answer the way they think you want them to." Even "did you find anything frustrating" is "kind of leading" (though it surfaces objections). "Don't name the feeling… extract the emotion." [0:56:43–0:57:44]
- "What did you feel" makes them pick a specific emotion; "how did you feel" gives a range (happy/sad). It's "building an association". [0:57:44–0:58:46]

### Rules of thumb
- **DO** one TEEP stage per campaign and one emotion per ad set; on a small budget, one campaign with an ad set per stage. [0:15:38; 0:39:49–0:40:55]
- **DON'T** put the whole TEEP model in one ad set. [0:16:41]
- **IF** evaluation-stage ads convert most **THEN** the market is educated: widen the top of the funnel. **IF** trigger ads convert most **THEN** nurture more. [0:21:52–0:22:56]
- **DO** map reviews to the nine mindstate emotions, keep the top ~3, and write ~3 nuanced ads for each. [0:36:36–0:37:39]
- **DO** name the precise emotion; broad emotions make ads expensive. [0:38:43]
- **IF** two emotions win separately **THEN** blend them into one ad and test it as a new campaign. [0:46:18–0:47:21]
- **DO** treat guarantees/promises as the security emotion. [0:48:21]
- **IF** the benefit is in the future (insurance, supplements, fitness) **THEN** make it near, concrete and specific. [0:17:41–0:19:46]
- **DO** consider a mascot ("fluency mechanism") for low-interest categories; try non-human, non-animal ones. [0:19:46–0:21:52]
- **DON'T** build acquisition creative only from reviews/post-purchase data. [0:23:59–0:25:00]
- **DO** check category health (Google Trends) before blaming the creative. [0:25:00–0:26:05]
- **DO** use AnswerThePublic question types to judge how much education the ads must do. [0:52:34–0:54:36]
- **IF** the category is uniformly aspirational **THEN** test Zone 3/4, **AND** end with hope. [0:29:15–0:30:17]
- **IF** a humor ad spikes and dies **THEN** keep "humor works", test humor types, and tie the joke to the product (product as character). [0:31:21–0:33:26]
- **DO** treat stars, quotes, badges, buttons and prices as supporting elements, never the core message. [0:34:31]
- **IF** buyers research for a long time **THEN** validate their savvy up front rather than adding discounts. [0:35:35–0:36:36]
- **IF** selling men's jewelry **THEN** lead with respect and durability; keep luxury signals subtle for Gen Z. [0:05:04–0:07:10]
- **DO** make retained customers feel special and surprised; don't rely on points. [0:09:15–0:10:19]
- **IF** selling an info product **THEN** sell one problem first through a small tripwire. [0:43:05–0:45:15]
- **DON'T** use "think" or name a feeling in survey questions; ask "how did you feel" open-ended, then "what would make it a 10". [0:55:41–0:58:46]

## How to apply
- [ ] Before briefing: Google Trends for category health; AnswerThePublic for education level; Creator Search Insights for under-served search terms; Reddit for raw emotion.
- [ ] Map our (and competitors') reviews to the nine mindstate emotions with ChatGPT; keep the top 3; split each into 3 precise sub-emotions.
- [ ] Brief ~9 ads per emotion, labeled by TEEP stage. Name ads so stage and emotion are readable in Ads Manager.
- [ ] When two emotions win, write a blended headline + subhead (ChatGPT blend prompt) and test it in a new campaign.
- [ ] For future-benefit products, write 3–5 near-term "horoscope" hooks; consider a product/object mascot.
- [ ] For negative zones, script the hopeful ending first.
- [ ] Keep stars, quotes, badges and prices as supporting layers in every static/UGC brief.
- [ ] Rewrite survey/interview questions: "how do you feel", 1–10, "what would make it a 10", no named feelings.
