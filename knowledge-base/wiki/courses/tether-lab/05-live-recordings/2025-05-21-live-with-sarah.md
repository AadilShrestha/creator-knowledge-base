# Live With Sarah! (May 21, 2025)
> Sarah explains her **TEEP** test structure (campaign = generation, ad set = emotion, each ad stack holds trigger, exploration, evaluation and purchase ads), then answers questions: B2B agency lead-gen copy (goal gradient, "you" messaging, separate pixels per country), retraining a pixel after a pivot (pick an enemy: introverts vs hustle-culture networking), high-ticket products (perception management; mattresses are bought for *waking up*), multi-brand wellness stores (the brain files you under "Amazon"), triggers vs motivators, 30+ women and supplements (validation), why "impulse buys" don't exist (identity stacking + humour), and making guilt ads ethical (the "mirror effect" parody).

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** planning a static test matrix that also reveals the buyer's journey stage; writing exploration vs evaluation ads; writing B2B or lead-gen hooks; choosing a brand enemy; repositioning high-ticket products; writing for 30+ women in supplements; adding a humour identity to novelty products; softening guilt/shame concepts.
- **Inputs → outputs:** product + target generations + 1–3 candidate emotions → a TEEP stack per emotion (trigger / exploration / evaluation / purchase statics, 10–15 per ad set) → read which stage converts per generation × emotion → scale that stage into UGC/video; plus hook rewrites (we → you, enemy call-outs, goal-gradient lines, validation lines).
- **Rules:**
  - DO put **trigger, exploration, evaluation and purchase ads inside every emotion ad set** (campaign = generation, ad set = emotion, 10–15 statics). Read which stage converts. [0:09:20–0:11:19] *(Contradiction: on [June 4](2025-06-04-live-with-sarah.md) she says one campaign per TEEP stage. See below.)*
  - DO make **exploration ads the weird, creative ones** (content people love for its own sake). Make **evaluation ads** about us vs them, ingredients, materials, manufacturing, founder ads, and Amazon/knockoff call-outs. [0:07:23–0:09:20]
  - DON'T run only trigger-event and purchase (discount/bundle/urgency) ads. The middle two stages are where creativity wins. [0:06:19–0:07:33]
  - DO test the method with **statics first**, let Meta pick the winner, then turn the winning message into motion/UGC/podcast. [0:12:09–0:12:56]
  - DO split countries by **pixel** (or at least campaign, with exclusions). US: price/quality comparison, sensational and bizarre creative. Australia: brand-loyal, needs a reason to switch. [0:14:41–0:16:16]
  - DO use the **goal-gradient effect** in B2B/lead gen: "You're 70% of the way there. All you need is a good partner to take you the rest of the way." [0:16:49–0:17:56]
  - DO turn copy from "I/we" to **"you"**. [0:17:56–0:18:57]
  - DO retrain an algorithm by **changing the creative** (it "doesn't need weeks… within a couple hours"). New pixel only if you must. [0:19:28–0:21:10; 0:27:42–0:28:55]
  - DO **pick an enemy** and make it the core of the marketing. It can be loud (Liquid Death) or subtle (Apple, Sarah's "logic"). [0:23:13–0:27:32]
  - DO sell high-ticket items through **perception management** and the real usage behaviour (mattresses → how you **wake up**, not "better sleep"). [0:29:31–0:33:05]
  - DON'T sell many unrelated categories on one site. Split brands, or give each category its own landing-page colour palette. [0:34:52–0:39:06]
  - DO **validate** 30+ women in supplements ("your gut feeling is right… you know your body best"). Nostalgia also works. [0:41:44–0:43:57]
  - DO stack a **secondary identity** onto novelty products. Humour (memes, puns, dad jokes, dry humour) is the most universal one. [0:44:25–0:48:44]
  - IF a concept shames the buyer THEN put the shame on **someone else in a story/parody** ("mirror effect"), or add a soft line ("no shame, we just want to get your dog what he needs"). [0:49:15–0:52:00]

## TL;DR
- **TEEP = Trigger event → Exploration → Evaluation → Purchase**, built from "a Google study" showing people loop rather than move down a funnel. Most brands run only trigger and purchase ads. The two middle stages are "where we can get super creative". [0:04:04–0:08:24]
- **Test grid:** campaign per **generation** (boomers / Gen X / millennials / Gen Z), ad set per **emotion** (e.g. esteem), 10–15 statics per ad set covering **all four TEEP stages**. The stage that wins tells you how close that generation × emotion is to buying. Exploration winners mean "produce more content". [0:09:20–0:12:56]
- **Goal gradient for B2B:** "You're 70% of the way there. All you need is a good partner to take you the rest of the way." Plus we → you. [0:17:12–0:18:57]
- **Pick an enemy.** Sarah's is "logic". For a Vancouver social blaster-games business the enemy is hustle-culture networking. Call out introverts and the socially awkward ("if you hate XYZ, this is for you"). The emotion it solves is **relief**. [0:23:13–0:27:42]
- **High ticket = perceived value.** "Marketing is about perception management." People don't buy mattresses for sleep, "they buy mattresses for… the experience of waking up". [0:29:31–0:31:41]
- **"Impulse buys don't exist."** The need sits in the subconscious for months or years. A triggering event links two identities (Sarah: psychology + mother → an instant $7 book buy). For tees, add humour as the second identity. [0:45:03–0:48:44]

### The four stages and their ads
| Stage | What the buyer is doing | Ad type (Sarah) | Examples / notes | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T: Triggering event** | An event made them want to buy | Talks only to the event | "You want to lose 30 pounds? Here's a product for you." "Do you want to get better at your golf swing?" Most brands stop here, then jump to purchase. | [0:05:41; 0:06:19–0:06:55] |
| **E: Exploration** | "What's out here, what products exist, where should I go, which brands offer this?" | The most creative, weirdest content | "Get water balloons and pop them all over and make them a paint… get a razor blade and try and shave a cat… stick [all the weird things in your brain] in this exploration phase." | **E: Evaluation** | Pricing, features, colours | Us vs them, proof of quality | Differences vs competitors, ingredients, materials, manufacturing, **founder ads**, "what we do best". ### Account structure Sarah uses (this call)
- **Campaign = one generation:** "our campaign for boomers… Gen Xers… millennials… Gen Zers… so I can understand which generation is actually resonating with us the best." (Sometimes other splits, depending on the brand.) [0:09:20–0:09:45]
- **Ad set = one emotion** (e.g. esteem). [0:09:45–0:09:58]
- **Ads = the "full roster", 10–15 per stack, all four TEEP types:** "I'll have trigger event ads, exploratory ads, evaluative ads and a purchase ad in every single bucket." [0:09:58–0:10:30]
- **Reading it:** "How quickly does this emotion get people through the funnel? Do we see boomers coming in in the esteem stack… really close to purchasing?" (purchase ads win). Or "our evaluative ads seem to do really well here… millennials actually resonate with [us vs them]." "One generation at a time, one emotion at a time." [0:10:30–0:11:19]
- **Q (chat): only a couple of the ads will get spend?** "Yes… that's okay with me." The winner tells you how fast customers reach the end. "If… all these exploration ads, the really creative ones, do really well, that usually tells me that this customer type is really resonating more just with the content than they are with the offer… then as a brand I need to produce more content." [0:11:19–0:12:09]
- **Statics first:** "Test this method with a whole bunch of statics… as soon as that core message is taking off I'll take that static and make it into something that moves, like an animation, a GIF, a UGC." Meta acts "like the filter". Then turn that stage into "UGC, pro video, like podcast… it's a repeat recipe." [0:12:09–0:12:56]
- **Jamie Ahmed (chat):** "A good thing to remember of why this works well: optimization is done at the ad set level." Sarah: most people optimise for conversions, which tells the algorithm to find people ready to buy today. "That only lets us dip into mid to bottom… You can't get new customers… If I want to get net new customers in, I got to figure out a way to teach [it]… go get a bunch of different people." [0:12:56–0:13:50]

### Q (attendee, marketing agency running FB/SEO/Google ads into Australia and the US): UGC and image ads aren't getting attention or qualified leads. What should we write? [0:14:20–0:19:06]
- **Multiple markets:** "The technical best way… would be to run **two separate pixels**", because cultures behave differently. Otherwise segment at campaign level with exclusions so each country gets its own set. [0:14:41–0:15:46]
- **US consumers (Sarah's generalisation):** "very consumption focused… price sensitive-ish… very quality heavy… focus really closely on how do you compare to everybody else." They like **sensationalism**, "odd creative, bizarre things". [0:15:13–0:16:16]
- **Australian consumers:** "not as price sensitive but they are very **brand loyal**… you got to show me why I should switch." Switching behaviour differs by country. [0:15:36–0:15:46]
- Everyone likes personalised messages: "the closer you can get to the identities… the better." [0:16:16–0:16:31]
- **B2B lead gen (marketing services for service-business owners):** lead-gen behaviour differs from DTC. Focus people on **how close they are to their goal** with the **[goal-gradient effect](../../../concepts/heuristics/goal-gradient-effect.md)**: "the closer you get to a goal the faster you'll try and get to it." Line: **"You're 70% of the way there. All you need is a good partner to take you the rest of the way."** It tells them "I'm actually doing okay. My issue is not me, it's just… I don't have a good partner." [0:16:49–0:17:56]
- **Turn messaging outward:** "We're the best marketing… we'll take care of everything" still shows skill, but "what we really love as humans is hearing about ourselves." Change "I/we" messages to "**you**" messages: "We help you drastically increase the growth of your business through solid marketing" → **"You need a growth partner that's going to help you take your business to where you want to go."** [0:17:56–0:18:57]
- "B2B needs to use [goal gradient] way more." [0:19:06]

### Q (Blaster Brawls, Vancouver social events): I pivoted my audience. How do I retrain my pixel without a new one? [0:19:20–0:28:26]
- A new pixel would do it, but retraining isn't hard. **The algorithm is a library:** "a mathematical statistical computer… taking what you have given it and saying *who is most likely to want to read this book*… I have a set of books on psychology, I need to go out and find a lot of people who like psychology." It tracks behaviour on Meta and a few site events ("like eight events, six events"). "You get to decide what's in your library." [0:19:49–0:20:40]
- "**You can retrain this algorithm within like a couple hours**… it's a computer, so it doesn't need weeks." Example: a pet-supplement ad running "50% off" was flipped to **"You're the type of pet parent who cares about what's inside their pet supplements"**, and that one shift from us to them caused an "instant change in the [account]". [0:20:40–0:21:20]
- **The attendee's business:** blaster-game events (*from the participant name "Blaster Brawls" and "the only place you can go shoot somebody and make a friend"*) in Vancouver, where "it's hard to make friends". Angle history: "Come here to meet new people" → "**You reclaim what the grind stole from you**" → wants to say "come here for the sake of having fun… as kids we used to just go and have fun". Sarah: angles like these "are very trigger events as well" and worked "for like 10 straight years". [0:21:20–0:23:13]
- **Pick an enemy:** "This is something that's really powerful for every brand in here: **pick an enemy**… and go after that enemy pretty hard." Sarah's enemy is **logic** ("humans base everything in emotion… you should not be marketing to logic"). His: **generalised networking / meet-and-greet events / hustle culture**. [0:23:13–0:24:20]
- **The emotion it solves:** "We're an incredibly lonely… species… we've put all of our effort into making friends online… real-world experiences… [are] abrasive… drive anxiety." So "you're taking social anxiety and making it easier for people to get… connection without having to… be confident. Emotionally what you're actually solving is… **relief**. You don't have to be social, you also don't have to be extroverted." [0:24:20–0:25:23]
- **New positioning:** "I'm allowing you to be an **introvert** or socially awkward and still get what you need as a human: connection." Ads shift "from *come have fun* over to *introverts, we're calling you out. People who hate hustle culture, come hang out with us… if you hate XYZ, this is for you*." [0:25:23–0:25:50]
- **Is an enemy too antagonising?** "You can also make it real mellow." Loud: **Liquid Death** ("we don't like corporate… anybody who wants general water, we're not for you"; Sarah also calls this "a fluency mechanism"). Subtle: **Apple**, whose enemy was enterprise-only computing ("we don't think you should have to be a billionaire to have a computer"), never stated, but "they put it everywhere". Sarah doesn't say "anyone who uses logic is stupid". She says "**psychology is how humans work**… if you… know that science is stronger than guessing, you're gonna want to come see this because it matches who you are." [0:25:50–0:27:42]
- **Just change the creative?** "Change your creative. You can train your algorithm within hours." Attendee's new line idea: "Remember when fun wasn't something [scheduled]…" Sarah: "**nostalgia**… a lot of Gen X with that." [0:27:42–0:28:26]
- **Chat (Jamie Ahmed):** Meta **Andromeda** has rolled out. It's "a lot easier than even 60 days ago". New pixels "shouldn't take long to train." Sarah: "I don't think we give the computer enough credit… it's built to adjust itself on an hourly… minute-by-minute basis." [0:28:26–0:28:55]

### Q (chat, Sohaib Emad, "Middle East"): Any winning angles for high-ticket products ($2k–20k) outside the US, e.g. pillows, mattresses, massage chairs? Do buyer behaviour and creative angles differ, or do the same emotional triggers hit? [0:28:50–0:33:05]
- "High ticket I truly believe isn't any different than selling low ticket. The only difference is the **perception of the perceived value**." A $50k car is worth it because "I believe there's no way I could build this car myself… a giant machine… a full brand." Examples: Maserati, Jaguar ("until recently when they tried to change things and they ruined their entire brand"), plus one unclear brand. *(Whisper: "blue baton". Unidentified.)* "**Marketing is about perception management.**" [0:29:31–0:30:28]
- Study which culture you're entering and its **perception of the product**. Every product has "a whole behavioral set around" it. [0:30:28–0:30:50]
- **Mattresses:** "Almost every single mattress company is saying the exact same thing: *get better sleep*. People don't buy mattresses to get better sleep… **they buy mattresses for the experience of waking up**. Waking up is a very painful experience for most humans… it has everything to do with how I woke up." The challenge is "to manage how people experience waking up". **Purple** (a past client) did well with "our technology is what makes the wake-up process better". [0:30:50–0:31:41]
- "The same emotional triggers will still hit… for similar products, but it depends on how they view the product." **Homework:** study real mattress behaviour: people pick a side "and never leave that side… for their entire life", sleep angles, blanket hogs vs no blankets, morning people vs night owls (circadian rhythm), and how each experiences waking up. [0:31:41–0:33:05]

### Q (Yana Zhurovich): I manage mattress brands and I'm now testing "wake up better" (reviews mention back pain). Separate question: a wellness client has several e-com brands plus one umbrella store selling all of them (vitamins, beauty supplements, hair dyes, all vegan except honey). The umbrella store gets little traction. Catalog ads, or duplicate the hero-product ads? [0:33:05–0:39:40]
- Sarah has seen this often: one hero supplement works, then "we're gonna make 15 more… men's health, women's health, kids vitamins". [0:34:52–0:35:15]
- **Category perception:** "Everybody has an association in their brain for websites… 18 different shirts… *this is a shirt site*." A site with supplements, minerals, hormonal health for men/women/kids "is all over the place… the brain is going to categorize you in [the] **Amazon category**", built for browsing like a department store. "The brain gets a little bit overloaded with options." But Amazon lets you find one category fast (type "cat litter"). On your site "hormonal health" returns one or two products. "You're competing with Amazon now, and Amazon will always be able to psychologically defeat you." [0:35:15–0:37:01]
- **Fix:** "I'd rather you **break the brands apart**… segment down your category so the brain doesn't have to think." Example: **Happy Mammoth** had mom, kids and men's products in one site and "had to split it apart because conversion rates just were in the trash". [0:37:01–0:38:03]
- **If you keep one site:** segment hard: each category's landing pages get their own design and **colour palette** within the brand colours (men's green, women's yellow…), "so psychologically you can start to break yourself apart". Yana's idea of positioning the store "for vegans" runs into the honey product. Sarah: it depends on what the brand will accept. DTC sites with "a bunch of products… tend to not do as well." [0:38:03–0:39:06]
- Sarah will do a future training on "how the brain perceives websites… depending on how you build the choice architecture". [0:39:06–0:39:40]

### Q (chat): Can you elaborate on motivators vs triggers? [0:39:40–0:40:44]
- **Triggering events** are "what you experience in your life at distinct moments": "I just moved", "I have a wedding coming up and I want to lose 30 pounds", "I just bought a puppy". They're your "real-world experience" and "pretty solid": "this event happened and now I feel like I need to go do something." [0:39:40–0:40:11]
- **Motivators are temporary:** "I didn't drink enough coffee this morning" or "I'm finally starting to heal from some of my childhood traumas… and I'm motivated to continue that journey". "Emotional motivators… wax and wane, they're very wavy." [0:40:11–0:40:44]

### Chat: "Ironic that Apple started out serving the common person but now you have to be rich to afford it" [0:40:44–0:41:44]
- Sarah agrees. Brands past a certain size "almost don't have to adhere to… the original of what you were going after… you've become familiarized in the culture". **Disney** (storytelling), **Pepsi**, **Coca-Cola** are examples. [0:40:56–0:41:44]

### Q (chat): Any patterns for marketing functional supplements to 30+ women, emotionally? Nostalgia is one. Anything else? [0:41:44–0:43:57]
- (Assuming hormone or holistic health and US-based buyers.) These women are "sensitive to… westernized messaging", in the child-rearing stage, old millennial/Gen X, "very independent", and "incredibly sensitive to information… they'll research a lot". [0:41:53–0:42:33]
- Nostalgia works ("this is the way it used to be… our health used to be easier to manage"). But the best: **validation**. "**Your gut feeling is right… XYZ is not serving you anymore**", "super crazy powerful", because doctors have told them "there's nothing wrong with you… here's some drugs". They've become "a little bit sensitive to authority… not sure who should I believe". [0:42:33–0:43:17]
- Say "you know your body the best. If you don't feel right, something's not right, and you should continue down that path. We want to help you." Offer a trial sample or a checklist. "Validation, validation, 100%." [0:43:17–0:43:57]

### Chat: Walker LeVan just bought a Purple [0:43:55–0:44:25]
- Sarah's Purple was bought by her husband. "Often it's the **husband that's actually the buyer and the wife is the consumer**", which is split purchasing behaviour. "A lot of dudes… are really interested in the mattress buying process." [0:43:57–0:44:25]

### Q (Joe): Which emotional responses (joy, nostalgia, rebellion) drive clicks, widen the top of the funnel or drive impulse buys? I sell tees with chaotic "trash-core" raccoon (trash panda) and cute kawaii designs. [0:44:25–0:48:44]
- "I'm gonna get a lot of hate for this, but **the impulse buy doesn't actually exist**… that need… exists somewhere in the subconscious for months, sometimes years, before they get to the point where they buy." [0:45:03–0:45:26]
- **Sarah's example:** an Instagram book on executive functioning for children, $7, from a creator she'd barely seen. A marketer would log an impulse buy, but "Sarah loves psychology… for decades… consuming", and a **triggering event** (having kids at 29 and 31) "caused me to connect two different things in my brain: my love of psychology and my love of my children." Now "any product that talks about nurturing the psychology of your children: instant buy." (She never read it.) [0:45:26–0:46:46]
- "You are going to have two different identities that are going to connect… based on which triggering events are on or off on the daily." [0:46:46–0:47:06]
- **For the tees:** find a **secondary identity** to connect. Easiest: "**I like funny stuff.** Humor is an identity and it works for almost every brand on the planet… highly universal." Use puns, dad jokes, dry humour. "Every time I've run a meme in an ad account it crushes." Start with humour rather than guessing whether moms or Gen Z like kawaii, then expand. [0:47:06–0:48:44]
- **Chat (Ahmed Almarri):** "I find it easier to do humor in organic content." Sarah: "100%… the cheap way… test it in organic and then pull it from your organic ecosystem [into] the ad account." [0:48:44–0:49:00]

### Q (Lesley Cramer): ChatGPT gave me a "what if your dog could choose" concept for my pet brand: disappointed dogs, guilt ("you've been choosing what's easy, not what's right"). I feel bad making people feel bad. How do I handle it? [0:49:15–0:52:57]
- "Technically you are not shaming. Technically it's the dog that's shaming." [0:49:49–0:50:00]
- **If the customer is sensitive to shame, wrap it in a story:** UGC where *other people* experience it. "A guy who just keeps passing his dog and the dog has a sad face every time he passes him in the kitchen, and he's finally like *oh okay I'll go buy it for you, geez, just stop looking at me like that*." Sarah calls this the **mirror effect**: "this is your experience… we're just gonna put it into a story so it doesn't get so close to you." Lesley: "just parody". [0:50:00–0:50:52]
- **Backlash risk:** some will find it rude. Negative comments aren't necessarily bad, but "somebody on the brand team is gonna come back and say… we need to take that down." [0:50:52–0:51:20]
- **Test version (static):** "You've been choosing the easy route. It's time to choose the right route." At the bottom: "**No shame, we just want to get your dog what he needs**," to balance it. [0:51:20–0:51:55]
- **Ethics:** "We are triggering psychology whether we know it or not, and I as a marketer would so much rather know what I'm triggering so I can very carefully make sure that I'm not doing it in a negative way… I would rather know what I'm doing instead of do it on accident." Walk the line and "see how intense you can get". [0:51:55–0:52:57]

## Rules of thumb
- **DO** build stage coverage into tests: trigger, exploration, evaluation and purchase ads per emotion. **DON'T** skip the middle two. [0:06:19–0:10:30]
- **IF** exploration (creative content) ads win **THEN** the audience wants content more than offers. Produce more content. [0:11:31–0:12:09]
- **IF** purchase ads win in a generation × emotion stack **THEN** that emotion moves that generation close to buying. **IF** evaluation ads win **THEN** lean into us vs them for them. [0:10:30–0:11:19]
- **DO** test statics first. Only build motion/UGC/podcast versions of the winning message. [0:12:09–0:12:56]
- **DO** remember conversion optimisation only finds ready-to-buy people. Use broader, more creative ads to teach the algorithm new people. [0:13:03–0:13:50]
- **IF** running several countries **THEN** use separate pixels (or campaigns with exclusions) and adapt for price sensitivity and brand loyalty. [0:14:41–0:15:46]
- **DO** use goal-gradient lines in B2B and lead gen. [0:17:12–0:19:06]
- **DO** rewrite "we/I" copy as "you" copy. [0:17:56–0:18:57]
- **IF** you pivot audiences **THEN** change the creative. The algorithm retrains in hours. [0:20:40–0:21:20; 0:27:42]
- **DO** pick one brand enemy. It can be loud (Liquid Death) or subtle (Apple). Name who the brand is *not* for. [0:23:13–0:27:42]
- **DO** state what the product solves emotionally (e.g. relief), not only the activity. [0:24:20–0:25:31]
- **DO** treat high-ticket pricing as perception management, and study the product's real usage behaviour before writing. [0:29:31–0:33:05]
- **DON'T** sell many categories on one site. **IF** you must **THEN** give each category its own landing-page palette. [0:35:15–0:39:06]
- **DO** distinguish triggering events (stable life events: moved, wedding, puppy) from motivators (temporary moods) when writing trigger-stage ads. [0:39:40–0:40:44]
- **IF** targeting 30+ women in supplements **THEN** lead with validation of their own judgement. Add a low-risk entry (trial sample, checklist). [0:42:49–0:43:57]
- **IF** a mattress (or similar) purchase **THEN** consider the split buyer: often the husband buys and the wife uses. [0:43:57–0:44:25]
- **DON'T** treat sales as impulse buys. Look for the identity pair a triggering event connected. [0:45:03–0:47:06]
- **DO** add humour as a secondary identity for novelty products. Test memes and puns in organic, then move winners into ads. [0:47:06–0:49:00]
- **IF** a concept shames the buyer **THEN** move the shame onto a story character (mirror effect / parody) or add a "no shame" softener, and expect some backlash. [0:49:49–0:51:55]
- **DO** keep using what works (cost caps etc.). Models morph. Put effort into creative. [0:53:20–0:54:02]

## How to apply
- [ ] For one product, write a TEEP stack for one emotion: 3 trigger-event statics, 3 exploration (weird/creative), 3 evaluation (us vs them, ingredients, Amazon call-out), 2–3 purchase (offer). Duplicate it per generation campaign.
- [ ] Tag each competitor ad you tear down with its TEEP stage. Note which stages the category ignores.
- [ ] Rewrite every "we" line in a script as a "you" line. Add one goal-gradient line for B2B or subscription products.
- [ ] Name the brand's enemy in one sentence, then write one loud and one subtle version.
- [ ] For high-ticket products, list five real usage behaviours and pick the painful moment (e.g. waking up) as the angle.
- [ ] For novelty products, brief a humour/meme variant. For shame-adjacent concepts, brief a mirror-effect skit plus a "no shame" softener line.
