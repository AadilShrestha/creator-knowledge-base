# 12.4 TikTok Ads
> A TikTok-focused (but platform-agnostic) system for paid short video: run a long avatar deep-research prompt through several LLMs, mine winning ads with spy tools using filters that separate real ads from viral flukes, produce volume with AI-generated and simple UGC-style creative, test four-plus new creatives every week, and iterate winners by swapping modular scenes instead of reshooting.

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** briefing, scripting or planning tests for a short-form video ad (TikTok first; the instructor says the same fundamentals apply to Meta, YouTube, Snap and others) [ov 00:00-01:20]; setting up avatar research for a new client or brand; reading spy-tool data (Kalodata, The Daily Virals) without copying a fluke; writing AI video prompts (Veo 3 vlog / interview / multi-scene); deciding what to test next week and how to split budget between net-new angles and iterations.
- **Inputs → outputs:** product URL, offer, price, existing winners (if any), competitor and category video data, customer comments / objections → (1) an avatar research doc run through several LLMs and loaded into one Claude Project per brand, (2) a creative-research note per reference ad (opening line, visual, overlay, format, tone, pacing, metrics), (3) a modular scene plan (hook, problem, alternative solution, our solution, proof, offer), (4) a weekly test batch of 4+ creatives and the iteration list for last week's winner, (5) AI-video prompts for fast concept tests.
- **Rules:**
  - **DO** research the customer before spending on ads. "Before we spend a dollar on ads" run the deep-research prompt (83 questions in the course doc). [v2 00:00:56-00:01:25]
  - **DO** run the same prompt through several models (Claude, Gemini, Grok, Perplexity). Each pulls different sources (Grok leans on X) and all "end up on the same person" but give different angles on how to talk to them. [v2 03:37-04:34]
  - **DO** put the research into a per-client / per-brand LLM project so idea and script requests start from that knowledge, not from zero. [v2 06:54-07:51]
  - **DON'T** copy the number-one video on a spy tool. Filter first: check the revenue trend for a one-day spike, set a minimum ad-view ratio so most views are paid, and cap creator followers. [v3 02:21-04:42]
  - **DO** take notes on each reference ad: opening words, visual, text overlays, POV or third person, length, tone, speed, raw vs polished, humor vs serious, plus the metrics. [v3 05:11-05:39]
  - **DO** build every ad's first three seconds on a stack of hooks (text + visual + audio) because you "take three shots instead of one". [v7 04:17-04:45]
  - **DO** re-hook through the video (mini hooks, call-backs to the opening) instead of relying on one opening hook. [v7 07:07-07:37]
  - **DON'T** run a hard "buy now" pitch on TikTok; use a soft, informational pitch with the link. [v7 10:00-10:32]
  - **DO** launch new creative every week, at least four new creatives per batch; aim for one winner per four tested (about 25-33%), expect one in eight to ten when starting. [v5 03:19-04:14]
  - **DO** treat creative as modular: change one scene at a time to turn one winner into many ads. [v6 01:27-04:49]
  - **IF** a creative wins in the testing campaign **THEN** raise its budget in place (vertical scale) and also add it to the scaling campaign. Don't turn it off. [v5 06:38-07:36]
  - **IF** the account is young (no winners yet) **THEN** spend about 75-80% of tests on net-new angles; later move toward 50/50 net-new vs iterations. [v6 07:39-08:38]
  - **IF** you must test several creatives in one ad group **THEN** make them near-identical (same video, different hooks); otherwise run one creative per ad group. [v5 04:41-06:09]
  - **DON'T** present an AI avatar or AI influencer as a real customer giving a testimonial or personal result. That is invented proof under this repo's rules (derived). The course uses AI video to test hooks, concepts, angles and looks, then hands the winner to a real creator. [v4 06:41-07:10, 11:58-12:23]
  - **DON'T** reuse competitor lines, scenes or the exact winning asset. Copy the structure only (see rule 3 in [AGENTS.md](../../../../../../AGENTS.md)).
- **See also:** [12.1 Meta Ads](12-01-meta-ads.md) · [4.10 Using AI to generate endless good hooks](04-10-using-ai-to-generate-endless-good-hooks.md) · [12.2 Native Ads](12-02-native-ads.md) · [Creative testing system](../../../../frameworks/creative-testing-system.md) · [Iteration vs variation](../../../../concepts/iteration-vs-variation.md) · [Test and iterate playbook](../../../../playbooks/test-and-iterate.md)

## Key ideas

### 1. The fundamentals are platform-agnostic [ov 00:00-01:20]
- Max Finn introduces himself ("Max Finn here") and says the module is about "really profitable TikTok ads", but the examples are TikTok-flavoured only; "these are fundamental principles to creating great ads". [ov 00:04, 00:00:00-00:00:26]
- His argument: "boundaries between platforms have quickly and rapidly dissolved, and they're going to dissolve even faster". What transfers is "what gets somebody to stop, pay attention" and "flip those switches in their brain to go from cold, I don't care, to red hot, here's my credit card". [ov 00:26-00:54]
- Even if you do not run TikTok ads, he says to go through it for Facebook, Instagram, YouTube and other channels. [ov 01:20]

### 2. Step 1: avatar deep research before any ads [v2 00:00-08:20]
- Two parts of research: your client / business / target customer, and what the competition and marketplace are doing. Most people skip the first or jump straight to spy tools (next video). "I think this is a huge, huge mistake", especially for agencies and new businesses. [v2 00:00-00:56]
- His standard pre-launch step: a deep-research prompt, "up to 83 questions", with a short context block: analyse website XYZ, understand product, value proposition, target market, pricing, positioning, then answer the questions. The prompt is in the lesson's docx and is reproduced in full below. [v2 00:56-01:52]
  - The questions come from "the last 15 years of running ads": the answers let him write more compelling scripts and a flow "from ice cold first view of an ad to red hot buyer". [v2 01:52-02:16]
  - Sections he names: demographics, pain points, goals and motivations, decision process, communication habits, current solutions, value perception, psychological triggers and emotional drivers, buyer psychology, media consumption, competitive intelligence, offer and conversion, objection handling, lifetime value, testing insights, advanced segmentation, usage and success patterns. [v2 02:16-03:37]
  - Lifetime value matters because the goal is turning a first-time buyer into a repeat buyer "so I can afford to pay more to acquire that customer". [v2 03:10-03:37]
  - He is "really big" on behavioural economics, consumer psychology and neuromarketing: most people "are illogical, not logical", so subconscious drivers are "almost like having cheat codes to marketing". [v2 02:42-03:10]
- **Run it through multiple models.** Claude Deep Research, Gemini Deep Research, Grok, Perplexity. Reason: "each one is going to be able to pull from some different data source" (Grok pulls a lot from X). All of them "basically end up on the same person" but give different perspectives on how to talk to that person. [v2 03:37-04:34]
- Output example: a Gemini run of "46 pages", including basic demographics and then "pain point solution mapping": the customer's daily self-talk ("I know I should apply for that promotion, but I just feel frozen"), the struggle (fear of failure, imposter syndrome), failed solutions (books, one-off motivational seminars) and the brand's solution. [v2 04:34-05:31]
- Then the research is turned into a plan: a pain matrix, core wounds, "everything is getting scored", done as "AI for that deep research and then also manually going through all that deep research and really pulling all these gems out". From there: angles, concepts, stories, scripts, shot lists, ads. [v2 05:31-06:54]
- **Storage:** one Claude Project (or Chat/Gemini equivalent) per client or business holding all this research (goals, drivers, pains, desires, psychological triggers) so brainstorming, ad ideas and scripts "[start] at the 20, 30, 40 yard line" instead of wild guesses. [v2 06:54-07:51] The on-screen doc for one client is split into tabs: Meta Ad Account Audit, Funnel/Offer Audit, Sample Talent Picks, Avatars (Grok / Gemini / Perplexity / Claude Deep Research), Angles, Hooks, Ad Copy, Video Scripts, Mini AI Brain (see screenshots).
- His diagnosis of weak ads: "they don't know who they're talking to... what drives them." [v2 07:51-08:20]

### 3. Step 2: creative research with Kalodata and The Daily Virals [v3 00:00-08:30]
- Preface repeated: the tools and formats work for Facebook, Instagram, YouTube Shorts, whatever you run. [v3 00:00-00:27]
- **Kalodata** ("the gorilla in the room"; Whisper writes "CaloData", the platform captions write "callo data"; the screen UI reads "Kalodata"): competitor, category, shop, creator, product and live-shopping research; the lesson focuses on the Video & Ad section. [v3 00:27-01:26]
  - Rank videos by items sold, revenue (GMV), CPA, ROAS and other metrics; break down by category, brand, ad vs organic, creator following. [v3 01:26-01:52]
  - **Read the trend, not just the rank.** Check the 30-day revenue trend: did it do well "because of some spike in virality" or "because it's actually a really, really good ad"? A video with one massive spike and a flat line is a bad copy target. [v3 02:21-03:19]
  - **Check paid vs organic.** Use the Ad View Ratio filter ("percent of views that came from the outside versus organic") and put a minimum there. [v3 03:19-03:47]
  - **Check creator size.** Filter to creators with under 50,000 followers so a mega-influencer's reach doesn't explain the sales; also require "at least half of the views came from paid". [v3 03:47-04:14]
  - **Pick the right category granularity.** Granular enough to be relevant, not so granular that you get ~10 videos, "especially if they're all bombs". [v3 04:42-05:11]
  - His failure case: people grab the number-one video unfiltered, copy it, and it bombs. "That's why." [v3 04:14-04:42]
  - Inside a video: watch it first, take notes on opening words, visual, overlays, style, POV vs third person, number of people, length, tone, speed, raw/organic vs polished, humor vs serious; then read metrics (views, items sold, revenue, follower growth, predicted ad spend, ad revenue, ROAS). [v3 05:11-06:05]
  - "Similar videos" creates a "rabbit hole" of related winners. You can also **download the video script** and drop viral winning scripts into the Claude / ChatGPT project used for ad ideas and scripting. [v3 06:05-06:35]
- **The Daily Virals:** same idea, fewer features: most viral content per day, filter by product, posting window, paid ads only (not "all"), gender, language; sort by total views, view growth, likes, comments. He watches the videos and looks for commonalities in length, style, pacing, tone, type of person and clothing; in his example a casual family, humor opening, kitchen, raw, no text overlays. [v3 06:35-08:05]
- Other tools he names: FastMoss and Foreplay ("all great stuff"). [v3 08:05]

### 4. Step 3: AIGC (AI-generated creative) as a testing accelerator [v4 00:00-14:14]
- The creative bottleneck: "how do I make enough creative, good enough creative to feed the machine." Media-buying tasks are being automated and rely on "high quality, high volume data and feeding lots of creative", so creative volume is the lever. [v4 00:27-01:23]
- **Veo 3 (Google).** Whisper writes "VO3"; the on-screen UI says "Veo 3 - Fast (Text to Video)". Eight-second hyper-realistic clips from a text prompt. Examples shown: a fictitious vlog-style influencer for a sleep-gummy lead (they build one for every sales lead to show the tech); interview / side-by-side, podcast, news and in-car styles; a multi-scene "vlog through the Revolutionary War" for a political-space company built from several 8-second scenes. [v4 01:23-04:29]
  - Two places to run it: the Gemini chat interface, or the more advanced labs.google "Flow" visual editor, which uses the same credit cost as the API. [v4 03:28-03:55]
  - Why he likes it: (1) "unlimited imagination, scroll stopping creative" without hunting a creator or a CGI studio (his examples: a kid fighting in the Revolutionary War with frostbitten feet, Sasquatch, "100 men vs gorilla" style vlogs); (2) fast, cheap testing of an angle or hook before paying a creator. [v4 05:13-07:10]
  - The scroll-stop definition he uses: "getting somebody to stop swiping and watch my video... that first three seconds". [v4 05:13]
  - **Result claim (instructor claim, unverified):** the Revolutionary War soldier video "was getting a two to three extra ROAS on multiple channels". [v4 11:34]
- **Prompt generators.** Their agency has prompt generators: paste a website URL, the model analyses the business, creates the persona and outputs a structured scene, who is in it, dialogue and technical instructions ("Don't include captions", pacing rules, "don't include products"). Paste the scene into Flow. Existing generators: multi-scene, interview/podcast, vlog. To make a new one (his example: a "day in the life" video that went viral on Kalodata), give Claude the existing generator and ask it to build a version for that format. [v4 07:10-09:34] The docx provided in this lesson contains only the deep-research prompt; the Veo prompts are not in the course files. Part of the Vlog prompt is visible on screen and is transcribed under Prompts.
- **Creatify** (e-commerce / direct-response focus): create avatars holding or using your real product (his example: a laundry-stain-remover bottle in a laundry room; none of it is real, "the product was recreated"), then a script runs across multiple scenes. Product-video types seen on screen: interaction demo, talking video, product shot. Upload a high-res product photo, it generates an image, then you click Generate video ("spin the product slowly to show all sides"). Other examples: unboxing with voiceover, a doctor in a lab coat holding a skincare product. Campaigns can also be launched from inside Creatify. [v4 09:34-14:14]
- Caveat from the instructor: "Is this going to be as good as a really talented creator? Not yet. It's going to be this within the year." Treat it as the way to "test way more cost effectively... hooks, concepts, angles, creators, looks, vibes" then "pinpoint your next creator you work with, your next internal shoot". Not every AIGC test needs to win; the point is volume. [v4 11:04-12:23]

### 5. Creative testing system [v5 00:00-07:36]
- The single biggest factor in success versus failure, from "so many" account audits: infrastructure to test creative, and a methodical testing system, plus a plan for what happens after a winner (iteration and "ascension"). The recording says "credit testing" throughout; the context and platform captions make clear it means **creative testing**. [v5 00:00-00:26]
- Example account (his client, a higher-ticket lead-gen business): "over $3 million" spent on TikTok, about $150,000 a month (instructor claims; the screen shows large spend in the testing campaign). Only two campaigns run: a weekly **creative testing campaign** and a **Smart+ (smart performance) lead-gen campaign** that receives the winners. Cost-cap / manual-bid and other scaling or test campaigns exist but are paused. [v5 00:26-01:53]
- Why it works: "we're fueling it every week with new winners, the performance stays the same." As platforms move to Advantage Shopping / Pmax / Smart+, they handle much of what advertisers used to do; for many accounts, "if you can just give the platform enough high quality data... and create enough creative on a weekly basis" these campaigns do well. He allows that manual control (CBO/ABO, own audiences, bidding) still has a place and "you can beat the platform in a lot of instances if you're really good". [v5 01:53-03:19]
- **Cadence and hit rate:** launch new creative every week on a fixed cadence (every Monday or every other Monday), "at least four new creatives". Target hit rate: roughly one winner per four tested ("batting between like 25 and 33"; the transcript says "250 and 330", the platform caption "two 50 and three 30", which he explains as one in four or one in three). Starting out: "one in 10, maybe one in eight". [v5 03:19-04:14]
- **Structure:** each batch gets its own ad group using the proven winning audience (here a broad audience); the winning audience becomes the control for new creative tests. The on-screen ad groups are named by batch number ("[AD Batch 1203-1207] Broad Audience...") and run $200-$500 daily budgets. [v5 04:14-04:41] (screenshot)
- **One-to-one vs batching** (the team does both): one creative per ad group gives total budget control. Batch several creatives in one ad group only if they are the same video with different hooks / setups, because then "we're not worried about too much budget going to one and a concept not being tested enough". Throwing very different angles in one ad group gives no apples-to-apples test and too little spend per concept. [v5 04:41-06:09]
- **Baseline vs outlier:** because they test constantly they know their average cost per lead is "in the teens"; an outlier that gets "$2, $3 leads" in the first week is the one to push. [v5 06:09-06:38] (the on-screen batch results show most ad groups between about $14 and $26 per lead, with outliers from $8.36 to $45.41)
- **Winner handling (two moves):** (1) vertically scale: raise the budget in the testing ad group, "if it's making you money, keep spending... push, push, push"; (2) ascend: add it to the Smart+ campaign (his example: it now holds 18 videos). Don't leave the winner untouched in testing or turn it off. [v5 06:38-07:36]
- Closing line: "we are going to treat creative as modular, not monolithic". [v5 07:36]

### 6. Modular creative and iteration [v6 00:00-09:34]
- Mistake: reinventing the wheel every batch. Every batch a net-new swing is part of the process ("that's how we find things like this video... getting $8 leads"), but "most people... find this winner, run it until they can't run anymore, and move on". [v6 00:00-01:27]
- **Monolithic** = one self-contained 60-second ad; to make more you must start over. **Modular** = a creative made of swappable scenes. His illustration (a Canva whiteboard) [v6 01:27-04:21]:

| Scene | Content | Swap ideas |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Intro hook | scroll-stopping hook with text overlay + visual + sound | different overlay, different visual, different line |
| 2 Problem | illustrate the problem | present the problem a different way |
| 3 Alternative / popular solution | what people try that fails | a different alternative |
| 4 Our solution | our solution and why it is great | different features/benefits |
| 5 Social proof | authority figure, customers talking | different proof |
| 6 Special offer | a unique, "crazy deal" for the channel | free shipping vs discount vs bonus stack = "three different ads" |
| 7 (optional) Final proof | stacked authority proof (customers, a doctor) | — |

  - He stresses it is "just one type of framework" used to make the point, not a template for every ad. [v6 03:23]
  - The maths: from one winning ad make 100 or 1,000 variations; at a 25% hit rate 25 of 100 win; "even if I'm doing a crappy job and only doing 10%" that is 10 new winners from one, "10X the scale, 10X the longevity". [v6 04:49-05:43]
- **Case: the "Look at the Tears" dental-implant ad.** A one-minute emotional ad for a dental implant business; at the end the patient cries. They took the crying moment, clipped it as the scroll-stopping opener, "so the end of the video becomes the beginning" (a loop format "really popular on the platform"). Next week's batch made several versions: that one did about $11 per lead (the original about $8) "still beating a lot of our controls". Another version used the face of the business (the dentist) on green screen (the transcript's description of what she talked about is garbled), combining green screen + authoritative face + the crying visual. [v6 05:43-07:10] (screenshots show the ad with the overlay "Full Upper or Lower Implants $8,999" and "Wait for the tears"; Ad Manager shows 722,425 impressions, 1,753 conversions, $8.36 cost per conversion for that ad group)
- **Weekly split:** every week do both: take big swings for new angles and iterate winners. Early on about 75-80% new swings; later about 50-50. [v6 07:10-08:38]
- **Flywheel:** learnings from iterations (green screen, duets, reactions, a certain type of person, etc.) feed the next net-new tests so new angles start from "the best possible place". [v6 08:38-09:34]

### 7. What the ad breakdowns teach (cross-ad principles) [v7-v13, BD4]
- **Length:** no universal best length; the question is "can you get the mission done in that timeframe"? Observed: 30-90 seconds is where many top performers land; 15-20 seconds for a $20 viral gadget; 2.5-3.5 minute VSL-style cuts for a high-ticket coaching offer. [v7 01:52-03:19]
- **First three seconds:** stop the scroll, spark curiosity, get them invested in a payoff. "If you can't do that, it doesn't matter how good your offer is." [v7 03:19-03:49]
- **Hook stack:** text hook + visual hook + audible hook, ideally not all saying the same thing ("a shotgun... three shots instead of one"). [v7 03:49-04:45]
- **Third-party framing beats brand voice:** "I don't trust you... you're clearly biased" when the business brags about reviews and doctors; the ad that says "I saw all these five-star reviews" sounds more authentic. [v7 05:14-05:43]
- **Visual change keeps attention:** cuts between POV and talking head and product; "boring direct-to-camera talk... would just not play on TikTok for most businesses". [v7 05:43-06:39]
- **Belief flip as an open loop:** "I thought this. A lot of people also think this. I was wrong." Viewers wonder why they were wrong. [v7 06:39-07:07]
- **Re-hook:** reference the opening hook again mid-video; mini hooks keep people watching for the payoff. [v7 07:07-07:37]
- **Alternative solution and alternative benefits + differentiating features** are modular scenes you can swap in and out. [v7 07:37-08:32]
- **Unexpected visuals = variable reward:** slot-machine analogy; do something the viewer does not expect, "especially if you're in boring industries". [v7 08:32-09:30]
- **Soft pitch:** price context ("usually this much, but you can get it for a lot cheaper") and a link, no hard sell. [v7 09:30-10:32]
- **Congruence:** match the setting and tone to how people use the product ("morning routine", unpolished) because congruence with real use "is going to increase conversion rates". Use a polished or luxury creator only for products where that is the right creator. [v7 10:32-11:29]
- **Props that make boring categories visual:** the BD4 supplement ad illustrates each problem with coloured-liquid bottles and Mentos, escalating bottle size; "nothing here is expensive". [BD4 srt 00:20-02:20]
- **POV and "mirror neurons":** shoot from the viewer's point of view (own hand placing the order in checkout) rather than a camera pointed at you; the instructor's analogy: watching a free-solo climber makes your palms sweat. (He states it as neuroscience; treat as instructor claim.) [BD4 srt 04:04-05:00]
- **Sell to both systems:** lead with emotional / illogical drivers, then add a logic block (ingredients, specifics) after the first call to action for the more rational buyer, then a second CTA. He cites Kahneman's System 1 / System 2. [BD4 srt 05:00-06:01]

## Steps / framework
The weekly TikTok loop, assembled from the lesson (the ordering is derived; each step cites its source).

| Step | What to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Avatar research (once per brand, refresh later) | Run the deep-research prompt (below) through Claude, Gemini, Grok and Perplexity; keep every output; score pains and core wounds by hand; save all of it in one project per brand | [v2 03:37-07:51] |
| 2. Creative research | In Kalodata and The Daily Virals filter to paid-heavy, smaller-creator, non-spike videos in a specific category; watch each; log opening words, visual, overlays, POV, length, tone, speed; download scripts into the project | [v3 02:21-06:35] |
| 3. Pick a format | Choose from the formats in the breakdowns: POV demo with hook stack, ASMR, reaction / stitch, pop-culture clips, retail comparison, comment reply, prop-driven problem/solution, AI-generated vlog | [v7-v13, BD4] |
| 4. Plan modular scenes | Write the six scenes (hook, problem, alternative solution, our solution, social proof, offer); for the hook write a text hook, a visual hook and an audible hook | [v6 01:27-04:21] [v7 03:49-04:45] |
| 5. (Optional) Fast concept test with AI video | Use Veo 3 / Creatify prompts to try a hook or angle before paying a creator; never present AI people as real customers (derived) | [v4 06:12-07:10, 11:58-12:23] |
| 6. Produce at least four creatives | A mix: net-new swings plus iterations of last week's winner | [v5 03:19-03:48] [v6 07:10-08:38] |
| 7. Launch in the testing campaign | New ad group per batch on the winning audience; one creative per ad group, or near-identical variants only | [v5 04:14-06:09] |
| 8. Read results against baseline | Compare to your normal cost per result (his baseline "in the teens"), look for outliers | [v5 06:09-06:38] |
| 9. Scale winners two ways | Raise budget in the test ad group; add the creative to the Smart+ scaling campaign | [v5 06:38-07:36] |
| 10. Iterate by swapping scenes | Change hook, problem, alternative solution, proof or offer one at a time; combine known formats; feed learnings back into the next net-new batch | [v6 04:21-09:34] |

### Rules of thumb
- **DO** vary the avatar (male vs female), time of day or setting when iterating a winning reaction ad. [v9 02:28]
- **DO** map the whole ad flow before filming an ASMR ad and exaggerate the sounds (tap hard, hold the razor close); amplify audio in CapCut or Edits. [v8 02:11-03:11]
- **DO** answer your top 10-15 "why haven't they bought yet" questions as a retargeting series. [v13 01:49-02:18]
- **DON'T** let the entertainment replace the sales basics: pop-culture ads still need a specific solution and benefits after the hook. [v10 02:04-02:34]
- **IF** your product's best demonstration is boring **THEN** add props or visual illustration (coloured bottles, POV checkout) so people keep looking while listening. [BD4 srt 00:00-02:20]
- **IF** you can't be on camera **THEN** use ASMR or product-interaction clips; sell the whole receive-open-use-result sequence without a word. [v8 01:43-02:41]
- **IF** you want to learn a viral format **THEN** feed an existing prompt generator and the viral example to Claude and ask for a generator for that format. [v4 09:06-09:34]

```text
The goal is creating SAMPLES that feel like authentic vlog moments

```

Agent note (derived): this prompt asks for "genuine discoveries or recommendations" from a fictitious vlogger. For our brands use the output as a concept test only; never publish AI-generated "customers" as real reviews or personal results.

## How to apply
- [ ] Before writing any TikTok script, run the deep-research prompt on the brand's real URL in at least two models; store outputs in one brand project. If the site is thin, ask for real customer language (reviews, comments) and list the gaps.
- [ ] For each reference ad record opening line, visual, overlay, format, length and tone; check revenue trend, ad-view ratio and creator size before treating it as a winner.
- [ ] Write the first 3 seconds as a hook stack (text, visual, audio) with three different takes on the same idea.
- [ ] Break each concept into modular scenes so one winner can be iterated by swapping one scene.
- [ ] Plan a weekly batch of at least four creatives; log hit rate against the one-in-four target and your baseline cost per result.
- [ ] Keep winners running, add them to the scaling campaign, and derive the next iterations from the winner.
- [ ] Use AI video only for concept tests; never publish an AI person as a customer with a personal result, and never copy the example ads' lines, claims or prices.
- [ ] For health, supplement, dental and weight-loss ads replace every claim in the examples with substantiated claims for our own product.
- [ ] If using celebrity, TV or creator clips, get rights and avoid implying endorsement (the instructor calls it "a little gray").
