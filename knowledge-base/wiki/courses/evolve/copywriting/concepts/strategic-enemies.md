# Strategic enemies (the ABCs: approaches, beliefs, competitors)
> Conflict needs an enemy. Every story ad and every position should point at one: an **A**pproach you compete against (powder greens, wire crates, Peloton), a **B**elief you stand against ("death to plastic"), or a **C**ompetitor. "When you define what you are against, it becomes clear what you are for."

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** a script or static has no conflict; choosing the angle for a story ad; tearing down a competitor ad to name what it fights; writing positioning; turning a product objection into a "real villain" line; planning an AI-animated "villain vs hero" iteration.
- **Inputs → outputs:** product, category, competitor list, what buyers resent (reviews), the brand's beliefs → one primary enemy tagged A, B or C, a one-line conflict statement ("their X, our Y"), and the hook or first beat that opens on it.
- **Rules:**
  - **DO** create conflict by choosing an enemy: "Stories equal conflict." [L42 00:00](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
  - **DO** classify it with Harry Dry's ABCs: Approaches, Beliefs, Competitors. [L42 03:40](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
  - **DO** ask: "What are you actually competing against? Who is your strategic enemy?" [L42 03:40](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
  - **DO** use the same enemy in positioning *and* in every story: "not only do enemies matter in your positioning ... they matter in your storytelling". Done well, it "will reinforce your positioning". [L42 00:00–00:50; 04:34](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
  - **DON'T** say "we're better" without a "why". Many brands "don't actually have the story around why you're better". "We're better than our competitors" is NOT repositioning; define a contrast. [L42 00:50](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L55 23:16](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
  - **DO** remember it is "not about hate". It acknowledges the buyer is making a choice. Avoid the "everybody trap". [L55 27:18](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
  - **IF** the enemy is a belief **THEN** phrase it as "we believe...", because it "resonates with identities and people buy based off of their identity". [L42 02:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
  - **DO** pair the enemy with something you say "no" to. "If you say yes to everything and everybody there is little chance of a strategic enemy." [L55 28:49](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
  - **IF** an objection is "these products don't work" **THEN** reassign it: "blame the real villain, not the category" (stomach acid killing probiotics, grain fillers). For an avatar objection, say "You're not broken, the villain is Y". [L28 15:48–23:27](../lessons/28-how-to-prevent-handle-objections-in-writing.md)
  - **DO** put the blame on an industry or hidden cause, never on the buyer. [L40 25:35; 27:56–28:52](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** make the enemy concrete and visual: "the Kardashians" beats "the internet". [L30 03:58–04:45](../lessons/30-how-to-raise-the-stakes.md)
  - **DON'T** fabricate or exaggerate harms to villainize a competing solution. Spencer called one villain-drug animation "borderline being kind of a piece of shit" [L53 03:58–04:48](../lessons/53-the-iteration-playbook-formats-styles.md), and the open-loop page's #1 rule is "DON'T. BE. A. PIECE. OF. SHIT." [L26 page text; 12:30–12:48](../lessons/26-how-to-write-open-loops.md)

## Definition
A **strategic enemy** is the thing a brand (or a single ad) is against. The enemy creates the conflict a story needs, and it tells the buyer what the brand is for. Spencer credits **Laura Ries** (*The Strategic Enemy*): world-class brands "all have a strategic enemy" [L42 00:00](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md). He uses Harry Dry's ABC mnemonic for the types [L42 03:40](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md):

| Type | Question (lesson page, verbatim) | Course examples | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A: Approaches** | "What approach are you competing against?" A different way of solving the same problem | Grüns gummies vs powder greens (AG1); client slide board vs Peloton ("you could bike all day long, or you could slide"); Kind Bar vs extrusion; Colgate vs tooth powders; Tesla vs gasoline vehicles; Uber vs taxis; The Farmer's Dog vs kibble ("brown burnt balls") | [L42 01:44–02:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L55 12:54–14:25; 27:58](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md) |
| **B: Beliefs** | "What beliefs are you fighting for?" A "we believe" stance | Comfrt vs mental-health stigma; Liquid Death vs plastic ("Death to plastic") and vs "boring ... yoga mom water marketing" | [L42 02:44–03:40](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md) |
| **C: Competitors** | "What brands are you competing against?" Direct competitors | Fable crate vs ordinary aluminum wire crates ("The competitors are jail crates, ours feel safe"); Lalo vs "ugly plastic high chairs"; Scope vs Listerine ("without giving you medicine breath") | [L42 00:50–01:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L55 25:04–25:47; 27:58](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md) |

Spencer notes the categories overlap: Grüns and AG1 are also direct competitors, but the ad fights the *approach* [L42 01:44–02:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md). Beliefs are "the open one", where many enemies end up [L42 04:34](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md).

## Why it matters
- **Contrast is easier than conversion.** "When you define what you are against, it becomes clear what you are for." Changing a firmly held belief is hard; contrasting a new idea with something already accepted works better. [L55 25:47](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
- **It drives growth.** An enemy "energizes and motivates" customers and drives word of mouth, referrals and organic growth. [L55 26:31](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md) (instructor/book claim)
- **It supplies the conflict a story needs.** In every L40 example "there's some kind of conflict and there's an enemy there". [L42 04:34](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
- **It handles objections.** Reassigning the blame to a real villain keeps the buyer's objection valid but aims it elsewhere: "You can't tell people that their objections are wrong." [L28 15:48–20:22](../lessons/28-how-to-prevent-handle-objections-in-writing.md)
- **It is one of the components the course audits.** In the 9-component breakdown of the Uproot washer ad, component 8 (Positioning) reads: "The enemy is surface level washing machine cleaners. Their positioning is they are the first-and-only to create an enzyme based cleaning tablet." [L47](../lessons/47-claude-code-10-step-iteration-process.md); [L52](../lessons/52-the-iteration-playbook-script.md) (DOCX tables)

## How to use it
1. **Map what you compete against.** List brands, features and approaches (the L56 worksheet tables). [L56 03:23–04:01](../lessons/56-positioning-exercise.md)
2. **Dig for the enemy with prompts:** "What made you angry that you had to create this product? (what is your enemy)" and "What do your competitors hope customers never figure out?" [L44 03:44–04:43](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md)
3. **Pick one primary enemy and tag it A, B or C.** The L56 worksheet allows "strategic enemy(s)"; for each ad pick one (derived default, see Cross-course notes). [L56 02:35–03:23](../lessons/56-positioning-exercise.md)
4. **Write the conflict line:** "Their [thing] is [bad, in sensory or concrete terms]; ours [does Y]." Model: "The competitors are jail crates, ours feel safe." [L42 00:50–01:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
5. **Add the "no" and a visual difference.** "Different beats better." [L56 02:35–03:23](../lessons/56-positioning-exercise.md)
6. **Open the ad on the conflict.** Choose the vehicle:

   | Vehicle | Pattern | Example | Source |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Story hook (market story) | Ask a question that exposes what the market hides | "What is the number one ingredient in most chocolate bars? ... It is not cacao." (enemy: sugar / regular candy bars) | [L40 22:46](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L42 03:40–04:34](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L35 18:08–19:32](../lessons/35-how-to-write-unaware-ads-hooks.md) |
   | Direct call-out | Name the ordinary alternative as the problem | "Your dog deserves better than a metal jail box. Those aluminum wire crates? Loud, rattly, and impossible to hide." | [L42 00:50–01:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md) |
   | Approach demo | Do the wrong method on camera | Grüns founder spits out green powder | [L40 07:49–08:47](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L42 01:44–02:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md) |
   | Mechanism villain | Name the hidden cause (enzyme, ingredient), then show the pain | "Scientists call it DPP-4. And it's literally breaking down your fullness hormone every two minutes." | [L71](../lessons/71-how-to-write-video-ads-part-2.md) (final script, Bridge 1) |
   | Objection reassignment | "Most [category] fail because [real villain]. These [work] because [differentiator]..." | Probiotics die in stomach acid; delayed-release capsule | [L28 17:52–18:50](../lessons/28-how-to-prevent-handle-objections-in-writing.md) |
   | AI-animated solutions or ingredients | Personify other solutions as villains and yours as the hero | "Hi, I'm fiber. And all I do is this... I don't actually help anything." (one of their top ads, "$500-600K" spent per the instructor) | [L53 03:58–05:36](../lessons/53-the-iteration-playbook-formats-styles.md) |
   | Hook template | "Never give this to your [PET/PERSON]! I'm [VILLAIN INGREDIENT/PRODUCT]!" | "Never give this to your senior dog! I'm Rimadyl!" | [L57](../lessons/57-100-hook-templates-form-winning-ads-deck.md) (sheet row 21) |
7. **Keep the enemy consistent** across ads so it reinforces the position. [L42 04:34](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
8. (derived) **In the brief, record the enemy as a field:** enemy, type (A/B/C), conflict line, proof that the enemy really does the bad thing. The L72 brief prompt lists "Introduce the villain or gap (enzyme, mechanism, hidden cause)" under **NEW** in its emotional-maintenance list ([L72](../lessons/72-action-item-write-your-video-ad-briefs.md), brief prompt; see [Value words: the Big 4](value-words-big-4.md)).

## Common mistakes
- Claiming "we're better" with no differentiation and no "why". [L42 00:50](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L55 23:16](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
- Trying to sell to everyone (the "everybody trap"), which leaves no room for an enemy. [L55 27:18; 28:49](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)
- Blaming the buyer ("you can't control yourself... don't say that, guys") instead of the real villain. [L28 22:57–23:27](../lessons/28-how-to-prevent-handle-objections-in-writing.md)
- Attacking the whole category when the objection is about one failure point. Reassign to the specific villain (capsule, filler), not "all probiotics". [L28 15:48–21:09](../lessons/28-how-to-prevent-handle-objections-in-writing.md)
- Naming an enemy in positioning but dropping it from the ads. [L42 00:00–00:50](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
- Villainizing with harms the evidence doesn't support. [L53 03:58–04:48](../lessons/53-the-iteration-playbook-formats-styles.md)
- (derived) Using a named real drug or brand as the "villain" (as in the "I'm Rimadyl!" template) without legal and compliance review. Default to the category or approach ("prescription pain meds", "wire crates").

## Cross-course notes
- **Agreement:** both creators say pick an enemy, make it core to the marketing, and use Liquid Death as the model. **Tether Lab** ([Brand enemy](../../../../concepts/brand-enemy.md)): "pick an enemy… and go after that enemy pretty hard". **Evolve:** every story and position needs a strategic enemy. Both also say: don't insult the buyer. Tether Lab: "DON'T insult people"; Evolve: "not about hate" and "don't say that, guys".
- **Difference 1, naming competitors:** Evolve includes **C: Competitors** as a legitimate enemy type, with named-brand examples in positioning (Scope vs Listerine, Shopify vs Amazon) and packaging call-outs (Jacob's, RXBAR) ([L42 03:40](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md); [L55 24:15–25:47](../lessons/55-why-you-need-to-position-your-brand.md)). Tether Lab advises picking "a belief, a habit, a category default, a culture, not a named competitor", and says to "question the trend, don't attack it" to avoid "scammy" competitor attacks. **Agent default:** in ads, frame a competitor enemy as a *type* or *approach* ("metal wire crates", "powder greens", "surface-level cleaners"), as Fable and Grüns do. Name a rival brand only for positioning analysis or with explicit client and legal sign-off. Logged in the contradictions notes.
- **Difference 2, how many enemies:** Tether Lab says pick **one** enemy, because too many blur the brand. Evolve's worksheet says "Define your strategic enemy(s)" ([L56](../lessons/56-positioning-exercise.md) worksheet). **Default:** one primary brand enemy; individual ads may dramatise sub-villains (an ingredient, an enzyme) that serve it.
- **Difference 3, volume vs taxonomy:** Tether Lab sorts enemies by *volume* (loud vs subtle); Evolve sorts by *type* (A/B/C). They are complementary: tag both (e.g. "B, loud" for Liquid Death).
