# Story building blocks (the five blocks and the storytelling laws)
> A story is conflict. In Evolve's teaching, every ad story that holds attention has five blocks: change, withheld information, stakes and drama, tension and suspense (beats joined by "but" or "so", never "and"), and cause and effect. It also passes the extra laws: it has people in it, it is ordinary and relatable, and it shows instead of explaining. Use the blocks to audit a competitor's story ad or to build our own.

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** tearing down a story-led competitor ad (UGC, founder video, customer story, static with a story headline); writing or QA-ing a story script; turning a review or brand claim into a story hook; deciding whether a draft is a story or a "shopping list".
- **Inputs → outputs:** a competitor transcript, our draft script, or a review → a block-by-block audit naming the line that delivers each block, the missing blocks to add, and a but/so beat chain.
- **Rules:**
  - **DO** add conflict to any flat line: "You don't have a story unless you have conflict." Example: "The man worked at his desk" becomes "The man worked at the **boss's** desk". [L40 00:00–02:12](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** build all five blocks in. "If you're writing your story and it doesn't have one of these five things, it's not going to be as powerful." [L40 11:12–12:16](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** open on a moment of unexpected change or an information gap, "and ideally both". [L40 17:07](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** follow the 80/20 rule: the hook tells 80% of the story and leaves 20% to work out. Leave out too much and people won't watch. [L40 14:00–14:53](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** join every beat with an imagined "but" (changes direction) or "so" (moves it forward). **DON'T** join beats with "and": "it's not a story. It's a SHOPPING LIST". [L40 19:20–21:10](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L41 03:33–04:29](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
  - **DO** put people in it: "stories are about people". The easiest route is to turn a customer review into the story. [L41 01:35–03:33](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
  - **DON'T** open with a big number or a brag ("billions of negative ions", "millions of shirts"). Start small and relatable: "It's easier to make something small feel big rather than the other way around." [L41 04:29–06:40](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
  - **DO** delete every sentence that explains and replace it with an example or footage. [L41 06:40–07:32](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
  - **DON'T** paste a raw review into an ad. Spencer says it fails "999 times out of 1000"; find the interesting line and give it a gap. [L44 05:34–06:20](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md)
  - **IF** the story can blame an industry, a hidden ingredient or "they" instead of the buyer **THEN** do it: buyers who feel good about themselves trust you more. [L40 25:35; 27:56–28:52](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DO** hold the product until the story pays off. In the Aviorra ad the pitch only begins after the story. [L40 29:46](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DON'T** rely on adjectives in place of knowledge: "In the absence of knowledge, you will be doomed to rely more and more on adjectives." (Bob Levenson, via Harry Dry) [L40 05:50](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
  - **DON'T** invent the story's customer. "Don't make up specific customer names/stories" ([L72](../lessons/72-action-item-write-your-video-ad-briefs.md), Borrowed Proof Protocol in the brief prompt).

## Definition
Evolve's story toolkit, credited mainly to **Harry Dry** and **Will Storr** (*The Science of Storytelling*). [L40 35:03](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L41 07:32–09:23](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)

- **Story = conflict.** "All writing can benefit from more conflict." [L40 00:00–00:49](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- **Position, story and storytelling.** Positioning = "WE do X". Story = "WHY we do X". Storytelling = "getting that WHY inside the customer's head". [L40 05:50–06:52](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- **The five building blocks** [L40 11:12–22:46](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md):

| # | Block | What it means (lesson wording) | Test question for an agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Change** | "Every story you'll ever hear amounts to something changed." "The threat of change is a highly effective technique for creating & keeping curiosity." (Will Storr) [L40 11:12–13:18](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md) | Is there a specific before/after or an unexpected moment, and does the opening hint at more change to come? |
| 2 | **Withholding information** | "Storytelling is the ART of withholding information." 80/20 rule. Curiosity is shaped like a lowercase "n": weakest at "no idea" and at "entirely convinced", strongest when people "think they have some idea but aren't quite sure". [L40 13:18–15:39](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md) | Does the hook leave about 20% unsaid through a question, a juxtaposition, a pattern break or a "secret"? |
| 3 | **Stakes + drama** | "The more DRAMA your story has, the BETTER the story will perform." "People are interested in the ABNORMAL, not the NORMAL." [L40 17:07–18:37](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md) | Is something big at risk (health, family, safety, humiliation, money)? See [Raising the stakes](raising-the-stakes.md). |
| 4 | **Tension + suspense** | "Create unresolved tension, and then resolve it." The gap "could be revealed at any second". Join beats with BUT or SO, never AND. [L40 18:37–21:10](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md) | Read the beats with "but" or "so" between them. Does any join read as "and then"? |
| 5 | **Cause and effect** | "Ads without cause and effect tend to be boring." Viewers should "insert their own feelings and interpretations"; "That 'insertion' of self is what makes stories so powerful." [L40 21:10–22:46](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md) | Does each beat cause the next, and can the avatar picture themselves in it? |

- **Three ways to bring in curiosity** (block 2): pose a question or juxtapose; do the opposite of the pattern; reveal that you learned a secret (e.g. "Elite marathon runners size their shoes differently than what the store recommends"). [L40 15:39–17:07](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- **The laws.** Harry Dry has five: no conflict, no people, no buts and so's, no ordinary, and "if you start explaining" (each "no story"). Spencer's "evolvified" list has seven: he adds **no change** and **no stakes**. [L41 00:00–01:35](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)

| Law | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| No conflict, no story | Add an enemy or tension ([Strategic enemies](strategic-enemies.md)) | [L40 00:00](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L42 00:00](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md) |
| No change, no story | Block 1 | [L41 00:40–01:35](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |
| No stakes, no story | Block 3 | [L41 00:40–01:35](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |
| No buts and so's, no story | Block 4. You don't write the words; they live "between the lines" | [L41 03:33–04:29](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |
| No people, no story | Put a human into the product; turn a review into the story | [L41 01:35–03:33](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |
| No ordinary, no story | Start small and relatable, then make it feel big | [L41 04:29–06:40](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |
| If you start explaining, no story | "Every time you explain something, just delete it and give an example." | [L41 06:40–07:32](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) |

## Why it matters
- Stories make information interesting, and most buyers need a lot of information ([High vs low information buyers](high-vs-low-information-buyers.md)). But "just telling a story is not enough"; early stories "just suck". [L38 04:45–06:17](../lessons/38-why-storytelling-is-so-powerful.md) The blocks are what make the difference.
- A story shifts beliefs without arguing: "Our job with storytelling is not to convince people, it's to get them to convince themselves." [L39 05:32](../lessons/39-why-you-cant-just-rely-on-education.md)
- A plot is only a container. "Just by writing a story a specific way is not what actually makes it work." The blocks do the work. [L43 00:00–01:40](../lessons/43-the-storytelling-arcs-plots.md)
- Spencer says applying Harry Dry's principles to his brands produced "more winners". [L41 07:32–09:23](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md) (instructor claim)

## How to use it
**Precondition: have something to say.** "To have a story to tell, you need to know more about your problem and your category than anybody else." Spencer warns that people "trying to replace a lot of the stuff with AI" will find storytelling "very hard". [L40 03:13–05:50](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)

1. **Find the raw story.**
   - Answer the six brand/market prompts (credited to Davie Fogarty): what everyone wrongly believes about the product; what would frustrate people; a story proving you're different; what made you angry enough to create it; what competitors hope customers never figure out; who it is NOT for. [L44 02:43–04:43](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md)
   - Or run the review-scoring prompt (verbatim in [L44](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md) and [Prompts library](../libraries/prompts.md)). It scores every review 1–10 on the same five blocks: CHANGE, STAKES + DRAMA, CAUSE & EFFECT, WITHHOLDING INFORMATION, TENSION + SUSPENSE. It flags extreme cases as [PRIORITY] and only notes what is missing for reviews scoring below 4. [L44 06:20](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md)
2. **Name the conflict.** Pick the enemy: an approach, a belief or a competitor ([Strategic enemies](strategic-enemies.md)).
3. **Write the hook** as a moment of change, a gap, or both. Leave 20% unsaid. Start ordinary, not with a brag. [L40 14:00–17:07](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md); [L41 04:29](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
4. **Raise the stakes** in the opening or the first escalation. Make the situation abnormal, and put a person in it. [L40 17:07–18:37](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
5. **Chain the beats with but/so.** Write the chain as a list first. The Conscious Bar chain is shown below. [L40 26:21](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
6. **Check cause and effect** so the avatar can insert themselves. Ask "how can I fit my product into my avatar's life?" and "how can I fit my product in a story that people will care about?" [L40 35:03](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
7. **Delete explanations.** Turn each explanatory sentence into a shot or example. [L41 06:40–07:32](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
8. **Pick an arc** for the order of the beats ([Story plots](story-plots.md)). Optional: test a hookless in-medias-res cut ([L27 19:44–24:24](../lessons/27-how-to-write-a-bridge.md)).
9. **Audit with the table below.** A missing block becomes a fix or an iteration idea.

### Audit table (use on competitor ads and our drafts)
| Block | Line in the ad that delivers it | Present? | Fix if missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conflict / enemy | | | Add an A/B/C enemy |
| Change | | | Add a before/after moment |
| Withholding (≈80/20) | | | Cut the answer from the hook |
| Stakes + drama | | | Use one stakes lever ([Raising the stakes](raising-the-stakes.md)) |
| Tension (but/so chain) | | | Reorder the beats; replace each "and then" |
| Cause and effect | | | Make each beat cause the next |
| People | | | Add the person, ideally from a real review |
| Ordinary / relatable start | | | Swap a brag for the small act |
| No explaining | | | Replace the line with a shot |
| Product late | | | Move the pitch after the payoff |

### Derived UGC beat skeleton (derived from the L40 teardowns; Spencer gives the blocks, not a script)
| Beat | Block | Pattern (derived) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Change + withholding | "I thought [normal belief]... [something just happened]" |
| 2. Stakes | Stakes + drama | "[Abnormal, high-stakes detail]" |
| 3. But | Tension | "But [unexpected fact]" |
| 4. So | Tension / cause-effect | "So [what the person did]" |
| 5. But | Tension | "But [doubt or obstacle]" |
| 6. Payoff | Cause and effect | "[Result the viewer can picture]" |
| 7. Pitch | — | "That's why [product]" + CTA |

## Common mistakes
- Writing a "shopping list": beats joined by "and then". [L40 20:03–21:10](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- Assuming the story is interesting because you're at its centre. [L38 06:17–06:57](../lessons/38-why-storytelling-is-so-powerful.md)
- Withholding too much, so viewers give up (too far left on the curiosity "n"), or too little. [L40 14:00–15:39](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- Opening with a brag or a big number nobody can relate to. [L41 04:29–06:40](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
- Explaining instead of showing. [L41 06:40–07:32](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md)
- Cutting "straight to the product", which Spencer gives as the reason the Fable crate ad is "not the best example of storytelling". [L42 00:50–01:44](../lessons/42-the-three-enemies-of-storytelling.md)
- Blaming the buyer instead of the industry or a hidden cause. [L40 27:56–28:52](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)
- Treating the prompts or the plot as the story: "these prompts don't mean anything" without conflict, stakes and the rest. [L44 07:18](../lessons/44-prompts-to-help-you-storytell.md); [L43 00:58–01:40](../lessons/43-the-storytelling-arcs-plots.md)

### Apparent tension inside the course
L40 says "people are interested in the ABNORMAL, not the NORMAL" [18:37](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md). L41's law "No ordinary, no story" says "start small" and keep it relatable [04:29](../lessons/41-the-laws-of-story-telling.md). **Default (derived):** start from an ordinary, relatable situation the avatar recognises, then add the abnormal, high-stakes turn. The grocery-store example does exactly this: an ordinary errand made abnormal by the ex and the girlfriend. The abnormal ingredient is the stakes, not a brag about the product.

## Cross-course notes
- **Evolve (Spencer Pawliw)** gives *quality tests* (five blocks, the laws, but/so) that any story must pass. **Tether Lab** gives *sequencing templates* (Pixar, Hero's Journey, PAS, In Medias Res and others) chosen by funnel job ([Story frameworks for ads](../../../../frameworks/story-frameworks-for-ads.md)). They fit together: pick a Tether Lab framework or an Evolve arc for the order of beats, then audit with the five blocks.
- **Agreement on setup and payoff:** Tether Lab's "two-thirds setup rule" (problem and lived experience before the product) matches Evolve's "product late in the story" ([L40 29:46](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)). Tether Lab's warning against "proof dumps" after the solution matches Evolve's "if you start explaining, no story".
- **Agreement on gaps:** Tether Lab's [Curiosity gap](../../../../concepts/heuristics/curiosity-gap.md) ("almost understand"), [Suspense](../../../../concepts/heuristics/suspense.md) ("tension through incompletion") and [Micro-story](../../../../concepts/heuristics/micro-story.md) say the same as Evolve's withholding block, its curiosity "n" and its stakes-before-story move.
- **Difference on how much drama:** Tether Lab's Micro-story says stakes don't "have to be dramatic—just emotionally sharp, awkward, or oddly familiar" and warns against a full origin story. Evolve says "the more DRAMA your story has, the BETTER the story will perform" ([L40 17:07](../lessons/40-what-makes-a-great-story.md)) and plays full founder and brand stories (Grüns, Liquid Death). **Agent default:** make the stakes real and specific first; raise the drama only as far as the product's true situation supports. Never fabricate. Logged in the contradictions notes.
