# Sight Smell (Show the Ingredients)

> **"In advertising sight smell means I need to be able to smell it through the screen… look at the ingredients of the product, smell each one of those in my brain."** ([Roast My Ads #1](../courses/tether-lab/05-live-recordings/2025-05-28-roast-my-ads-1.md) [0:06:19]). For anything you eat, drink or put on your body, show the raw ingredients (or the sensory proof) so the viewer's brain simulates the experience, and "unique" never has to be said.

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** briefing food, beverage, supplement, skincare or apparel creative; tearing down a competitor static that shows only a finished product on a plate; deciding what goes in the frame next to the product.
- **Inputs → outputs:** product + its distinctive ingredients/materials (named) → a shot list (ingredients beside the product, in-motion texture shots), proprietary names, and copy that no longer needs the words "unique", "real ingredients" or "made fresh".
- **Rules:**
  - DO put the ingredients on one side of the frame and the finished product on the other. "Don't necessarily show the product so much, let's show the actual ingredients." [Roast #1 0:06:19]
  - DO show, don't tell: "If you show me beef tallow caramel and bacon in an image, I'm gonna know automatically that that's a very unique flavor. You don't even need to say it." [Roast #1 0:07:22]
  - DO replace generic claims ("real ingredients", "made fresh", "fun flavors") with ownable specifics, and give flavours/blends **proprietary names** (Ben & Jerry's "Chunky Monkey"). [Roast #1 0:05:15–0:06:19; 0:08:26]
  - DO keep the ingredients visible in iterations of a winning food static (Aazar kept "the spices on display" and the outcome visual of the drink). [July 9 0:30:15–0:31:33]
  - DO make the food look appetizing **to the human**, even in pet products. [Roast #3 0:37:20]
  - DO, for apparel, use the sensory equivalent: fabric moving on a body proves quality, weight and drape; describe the feel with a sensory verb ("hug"). [Roast #2 0:09:29; 0:45:13]
  - DON'T cite the likability lift as a fact in client decks: the "13.6%" figure is Sarah's recollection of a study she tweeted, uncited. [Roast #1 0:07:07]

## Definition
Sarah's term for imagery that lets the viewer "smell it through the screen": the ingredients (or raw materials) are shown so the brain mentally tastes or feels each one. She paired it with a study she had posted that day: "you can increase likability of your ads by I think it was 13.6%" (no source given) ([Roast My Ads #1](../courses/tether-lab/05-live-recordings/2025-05-28-roast-my-ads-1.md) [0:06:19–0:07:22]).

It is the visual form of "show, don't tell": "Show don't tell, especially when it comes to digestible products." [0:07:43]

## Why it matters
- Generic food claims are claims "our competitors can probably claim too" [0:05:15]. Ingredients on screen are specific and ownable (see [specificity](heuristics/specificity.md)).
- The image is processed before the text ([Roast #3](../courses/tether-lab/05-live-recordings/2025-07-23-roast-my-ads-3.md) [0:32:37]), so the ingredient shot does the persuading in the 0.2–0.5 s the viewer gives a static.
- It triggers the [affect heuristic](heuristics/affect-heuristic.md): the viewer feels the taste before reading anything.

## How to use it
1. List the product's 2–4 most distinctive ingredients or materials (the ones a competitor can't claim).
2. Name them (and, if you can, give the blend a proprietary name).
3. Build the frame: ingredients on one side, product on the other (static) or ingredient close-ups before the product reveal (video).
4. Cut every word the image now says ("unique", "real", "fresh").
5. Keep the ingredient elements constant when you iterate headlines (they are part of what makes the control win).

## Deploy in a UGC script
| Script slot | Pattern | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| Hook (visual) | Slow close-up of each raw ingredient before the product appears | (b-roll: caramel pour, candied bacon crumble) "Beef-tallow caramel. Candied bacon. In gelato." (illustrative, not from course) |
| Demo | Creator assembles or tastes with ingredients visible on the counter | "This is what's actually in it." (illustrative, not from course) |
| Proof | Name the ingredient, not the claim | "Real vanilla bean, not 'vanilla flavor'." (illustrative, built on Roast #1 [0:09:27]) |
| Apparel version | Fabric moving on the creator; hands pulling the stretch | "Look how far this stretches. It hugs, it doesn't squeeze." (illustrative, built on Roast #2 [0:44:25–0:45:13]) |

**Visual cues:** split frame (ingredients | product), macro shots, steam/drips/texture, spices labelled around a cup, food that looks appetizing to people, fabric in motion.

## Deploy in a static
- Ingredients left, product right, short headline on top (≤7 words; see [visual weight hierarchy](../frameworks/visual-weight-hierarchy.md)).
- Labelled ingredient ring around the cup/bowl (Aazar's chai control: cinnamon, nutmeg, turmeric visible).

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Are raw ingredients or materials in frame, or only the finished product? Product-only = a gap to exploit.
- Generic claim chips ("real ingredients", "no fake stuff", "made fresh") = telling instead of showing.
- Named proprietary flavours/blends = they understand naming.
- Tags: `visual-sight-smell` (ingredients shown), `visual-product-only` (gap).

## Rules of thumb
- **DO** show ingredients more than the product for food. [Roast #1 0:06:53]
- **DO** give flavours and blends proprietary names. "Naming is incredibly important for every brand on the planet." [Roast #1 0:08:26]
- **IF** apparel **THEN** show it moving on a person. [Roast #2 0:09:33]
- **DON'T** say "unique" when the image can show it. [Roast #1 0:07:22]

## Common mistakes
- Pretty product flat-lay with nothing to "smell".
- Ingredient shot that contradicts the headline (see [hero image ↔ copy congruence](hero-image-copy-congruence.md)).
- Quoting "+13.6% likability" as a verified stat.
