# Salience Bias

> **"Whatever stands out gets the brain's attention. Novelty and contrast force a pause."** (e-book, p.13). Shortcut #7 in [Heuristics 101](../../courses/tether-lab/02-heuristics-101/README.md). This is the attention/thumb-stop heuristic: "shiny quarter syndrome."

## 🤖 Agent use
- **Use when:** designing the first frame or thumbnail, picking background/product colours, briefing creators on set and wardrobe, or diagnosing why a competitor ad stops the scroll.
- **Inputs → outputs:** product colour and brand palette → a contrast plan (calm field + one drastic focal object) and first-frame direction.
- **Rules:**
  - DO make everything around one colour (white space, grey, beige) and put one drastically different object **dead centre** (red, orange, black).
  - DO use odd formats, odd objects or unexpected visuals for novelty.
  - DON'T use all-muted palettes. "Muted colors... tend to kind of wash together."
  - IF the background is vibrant THEN make the product a single dark standalone object in the centre (Sarah's Firelight Flask fix).
- **See also:** [Processing fluency](processing-fluency.md) (calm background = fluent), [Heuristic stacking](../heuristic-stacking.md), [Affect heuristic](affect-heuristic.md).

## Definition
"We have **shiny quarter syndrome** as humans... What stands out captures a whole lot of attention" ([L4 00:39]–[00:49]).

## Why it works
"Novelty really does trigger awareness for the brain. It flags it as important" ([L4 00:50]–[00:54]).

## How to use it in ads (from the course)
- **Best version:** "make everything around one color, like white space, and then something dead in the center, another color that's pretty drastic. So red, orange, black" ([L4 01:22]–[01:34]). Repeated in lesson 5: "very, very... calm background, say like a white or a gray background with something vibrant in the center, like a red ball, blue ball" ([L5 04:48]–[05:03]).
- **Alternative:** a bright outer colour broken up by darker colours inside, which pulls the eye to the centre ([L4 00:56]–[01:19]).
- **Applications:** "very visual contrast, like bold colors, odd formats, odd objects"; "something that's strange, odd, or... a little bit different color... Contrast between two things" ([L4 01:47]–[02:07]).
- **E-book:** "Bright, bold colors against muted backgrounds · Unexpected visuals · Clear focal points." Pro tip: "**In a sea of beige, be the red balloon.**"
- "Anything distinctive inside the ad is going to get the most attention" ([L5 05:17]–[05:26]).

## Deploy in a UGC script
*Derived guidance: the course covers statics. These video adaptations are not from the course.*

| Script slot | Pattern |
|---|---|
| First frame / thumbnail (0–1 s) | One high-contrast object centred against a plain backdrop (a red product on a white wall; a black bottle on beige) |
| Creator brief | Wardrobe and background in one muted tone so the product is the only saturated element |
| Pattern interrupt | Odd object or odd format in the opening shot (an unexpected prop, an unusual crop) |
| On-screen sticker | One bright sticker (e.g. a red circle) on a calm frame |

## Spot it in a competitor ad
- Look at the first frame: is there one obvious focal point? What colour contrast creates it?
- Check for a bright sticker or badge on a muted layout, or an odd object or format.
- Note an **inverted** salience (vibrant background, dull product) as a weakness we can fix in our remix.

## Rules of thumb
- **DO** use a calm single-colour background + one drastic centre object ([L4 01:22]).
- **DON'T** rely on muted colours. They wash together ([L4 01:38]).
- **IF** the background is busy or vibrant **THEN** isolate one dark product in the centre ([L5 05:09]).
- **DO** "In a sea of beige, be the red balloon." (e-book p.13)
